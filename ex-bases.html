Conversion in every direction

Positional notation in an arbitrary base; converting to decimal by expansion and from decimal by repeated division; and the grouping shortcut that makes binary, quaternary, octal and hexadecimal four ways of writing the same thing.

There is only one number here. 182, 10110110, 23124, 2668 and 0xB6 are five notations for a single quantity, and none of them is more real than the others. The skill this set drills is moving between the notations quickly and without a calculator, because from Lecture 3 onward every wire, register and address in the course is written in one of them.

2.1What a base means

In base b, the digit in position i (counting from 0 at the right) is worth bi times its face value. Nothing else is going on. The number written d3d2d1d0 in base b is

d3b3 + d2b2 + d1b1 + d0b0

Two consequences worth stating out loud, because most conversion errors are really errors about one of them:

  • A base-b system has exactly b digit symbols, running from 0 to b − 1. There is no digit b itself — no 2 in binary, no 8 in octal. When a base needs more than ten symbols, letters take over: A is 10, B is 11, and so on.
  • The value of a number does not depend on the base; only the notation does. "Converting to binary" changes how you write it, not what it is.
Notation

Where the base is not obvious this page writes it as a subscript: 10112. Hexadecimal also appears in the C and MIPS style, 0x prefix, since that is what you will see in MARS and in Logisim. Binary sometimes takes a 0b prefix for the same reason.

2.2Base b to decimal

Method: positional expansion. Write the place values above the digits, multiply, add. This works for every base and is the one to reach for when the base is not a power of two.

3122 in base 4, to decimal
place value43 = 6442 = 1641 = 440 = 1
digit3122
product1921682

192 + 16 + 8 + 2 = 218

Faster method for binary: doubling. Start at 0, and for each bit left to right, double the running total and add the bit. For 10110110: 1, 2, 5, 11, 22, 45, 91, 182. No place values to write down, no powers to remember, and it is much harder to lose your place. It works in any base — multiply by b instead of 2 — but binary is where it pays.

Two things to watch

Bottom to top. Reading the remainders top to bottom gives you the number backwards, and it will often still look plausible. Always finish with a check — expand your answer back to decimal, or double it back.

Remainders of ten or more. In base 12 or 16 a remainder can be 10, 11, 12… and each is a single digit: 10 is A, not "1 0". Writing 2026 in base 12 as 12010 instead of 120A is the classic version of this mistake.

2.4The power-of-two shortcut

When one base is a power of another, conversion is pure regrouping — no arithmetic at all. Since 4 = 22, 8 = 23 and 16 = 24:

to convert binary to…group bits from the right in…
quaternary (base 4)2s
octal (base 8)3s
hexadecimal (base 16)4s

Pad the leftmost group with leading zeros if it is short, translate each group independently, and you are done. The reverse direction is the same in reverse: expand each digit into its 2, 3 or 4 bits, keeping the leading zeros this time.

One binary number, three ways

Take 1101101001112.

groupeddigitsresult
11 01 10 10 01 113 1 2 2 1 33122134
110 110 100 1116 6 4 766478
1101 1010 011113 10 70xDA7

All four notations denote 3495. Only the last line needed the letter digits: 13 is D and 10 is A.

Why hexadecimal and not quaternary?

Quaternary halves the length of a binary string; hexadecimal cuts it to a quarter, and a byte becomes exactly two digits. That last fact is why hexadecimal won: memory addresses, machine instructions and colour codes all come in whole bytes, and hexadecimal lines up with byte boundaries where octal does not. Octal survives in Unix file permissions, where the natural grouping really is three bits at a time.

2.6Exercises

No calculator. Exercises 1–9 are the standard conversions, 10–12 push into bases you have not used, and 13–15 ask you to reason about bases rather than convert in them.

To decimal and back

Convert 101101102 to decimal, by positional expansion and again by doubling.

Expansion. The 1s sit at positions 7, 5, 4, 2 and 1:

128 + 32 + 16 + 4 + 2 = 182

Doubling, left to right, starting from 0: 1, 2, 5, 11, 22, 45, 91, 182. Same answer, and no place values written down.

Convert 110101112 to decimal. Then convert your answer back to binary by repeated division and check you recover the same string.

1s at positions 7, 6, 4, 2, 1, 0: 128 + 64 + 16 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 215. By doubling: 1, 3, 6, 13, 26, 53, 107, 215. Agreed.

Back: 215 → 107 r1, 107 → 53 r1, 53 → 26 r1, 26 → 13 r0, 13 → 6 r1, 6 → 3 r0, 3 → 1 r1, 1 → 0 r1. Bottom to top: 11010111. The string we started with.

Convert 2026 to binary by repeated division, then say how many bits it needs and why that number is what it is.

The division chain is worked through in full in §2.3; the answer is 111111010102, eleven bits.

Eleven because 210 = 1024 ≤ 2026 < 2048 = 211. In general a number N needs the smallest n with 2n > N, and the leading bit is the one worth 2n−1.

Using the binary from Exercise 2.3, write 2026 in quaternary, octal and hexadecimal — without dividing again.

Convert 0x3F5 to decimal and to binary.

Decimal. Place values 256, 16, 1; F is 15:

3×256 + 15×16 + 5 = 768 + 240 + 5 = 1013

Binary. Expand each digit into four bits — 3 is 0011, F is 1111, 5 is 0101:

0011 1111 0101 → 1111110101

Ten bits once the two leading zeros are dropped. Cross-check by adding the place values of the 1s: 512+256+128+64+32+16+4+1 = 1013. Agreed.

Convert 7258 to binary, to hexadecimal and to decimal.

Binary, three bits per octal digit: 7 is 111, 2 is 010, 5 is 101, giving 1110101012.

Hexadecimal, regrouping the same bits in fours from the right: 0001 1101 0101 → 1, D, 5 → 0x1D5.

Decimal: 7×64 + 2×8 + 5 = 448 + 16 + 5 = 469. Check against the hex: 1×256 + 13×16 + 5 = 256 + 208 + 5 = 469.

Note what did not happen: converting octal to hexadecimal never went through decimal. Binary is the bridge between any two power-of-two bases.

Quaternary and mixed grouping

Convert 31224 to decimal, to binary and to hexadecimal.

Decimal (worked in §2.2): 192 + 16 + 8 + 2 = 218.

Binary, two bits per quaternary digit: 3 is 11, 1 is 01, 2 is 10, 2 is 10, giving 110110102.

Hexadecimal, regrouping in fours: 1101 1010 → D, A → 0xDA. Check: 13×16 + 10 = 218.

Eight bits, so exactly one byte, so exactly two hex digits — and exactly four quaternary digits. Every power-of-two base divides a byte evenly except octal.

Convert 1101101001112 to quaternary, octal and hexadecimal, and give its decimal value.

The three groupings are worked in §2.4: 3122134, 66478, 0xDA7.

Decimal, most easily from the hexadecimal: 13×256 + 10×16 + 7 = 3328 + 160 + 7 = 3495.

Doing it from the binary directly means adding 2048+1024+256+128+32+4+2+1, which is the same 3495 with eight more chances to slip. Convert to hex first, then expand: it is almost always the shorter road.

Convert 0xBEEF to quaternary without going through decimal.

Each hexadecimal digit is four bits, which is exactly two quaternary digits. So translate one hex digit at a time into a two-digit quaternary pair:

(For the record it is 48879 in decimal, but you never needed that.) The general rule: whenever one base is a power of the other, conversion is regrouping, and regrouping never requires arithmetic.

Unfamiliar bases

Write 240 in base 3, base 5 and base 7.

Base 3. 240 → 80 r0, 80 → 26 r2, 26 → 8 r2, 8 → 2 r2, 2 → 0 r2. Bottom to top: 222203. Check: 2×81 + 2×27 + 2×9 + 2×3 + 0 = 162+54+18+6 = 240.

Base 5. 240 → 48 r0, 48 → 9 r3, 9 → 1 r4, 1 → 0 r1 → 14305. Check: 125 + 4×25 + 3×5 + 0 = 125+100+15 = 240.

Base 7. 240 → 34 r2, 34 → 4 r6, 4 → 0 r4 → 4627. Check: 4×49 + 6×7 + 2 = 196+42+2 = 240.

Notice the lengths: the smaller the base, the more digits. 240 takes five digits in base 3, four in base 5, three in base 7 — and eight in binary, 11110000.

Write 2026 in base 12, using A for ten and B for eleven.

2026 → 168 r10, 168 → 14 r0, 14 → 1 r2, 1 → 0 r1.

Reading bottom to top, with 10 written as the single digit A:

2026 = 120A12

Check: 1×1728 + 2×144 + 0×12 + 10 = 1728 + 288 + 0 + 10 = 2026.

This is the exercise where the trap in §2.3 bites. The first remainder is ten, one digit, not the two characters "10".

The number written 31 in some base b equals 25 in decimal. What is b? Is the answer unique?

Positional notation gives an equation:

3b + 1 = 25  →  3b = 24  →  b = 8

So 318 = 25. It is unique: the left-hand side is strictly increasing in b, so at most one base can work. The one extra thing to check is that the digits are legal in the base you found — here the digits are 3 and 1, both less than 8, so the answer stands. If the equation had produced b = 3 with a digit 3 in the number, there would be no solution at all.

Reasoning about bases

What is the largest number you can write with four digits in base 7? Give it in base 7 and in decimal, then state the general rule for n digits in base b.

Every digit at its maximum, b − 1 = 6: 66667.

In decimal that is 6×343 + 6×49 + 6×7 + 6 = 2058 + 294 + 42 + 6 = 2400.

The shortcut: the largest n-digit number in base b is bn − 1, because the next number up is 1 followed by n zeros. Here 74 = 2401, so the answer is 2400 in one step.

The familiar case is binary: the largest n-bit value is 2n − 1, which is why an 8-bit unsigned byte tops out at 255 and a 32-bit word at 4294967295.

Give the next number after each of these, staying in the same base: 11112, 7778, 0x3FF. What do the three have in common?

11112 + 1 = 100002 (15 + 1 = 16)
7778 + 1 = 10008 (511 + 1 = 512)
0x3FF + 1 = 0x400 (1023 + 1 = 1024)

All three are the odometer rolling over: every digit is at its maximum, so adding one carries all the way and produces a 1 followed by zeros. And in all three cases the value is a power of two — 16, 512, 1024 — because each base is itself a power of two. 0x400 = 210 = 1024 is worth memorising; it is the "kilo" in kilobyte.

Challenge. Show that the number written 121 is a perfect square in every base b ≥ 3. Verify it for b = 5, 8 and 16.

By positional expansion, whatever the base,

121b = b2 + 2b + 1 = (b + 1)2

which is a square for every b. The condition b ≥ 3 is only there to make the digits legal: base 2 has no digit 2.

basevalue= (b+1)2
525 + 10 + 1 = 3662
864 + 16 + 1 = 8192
16256 + 32 + 1 = 289172

The same argument explains why 100b is always b2 and 11b is always b + 1: a digit string is a polynomial in the base, and any identity between polynomials holds in every base at once. That is a genuinely useful way to think about positional notation, and it is worth carrying into the next set.

Going further

Everything above stops at the radix point. Fractions work the same way — the digit in position −1 is worth b−1 — but with one twist that matters enormously later: a fraction with a terminating expansion in one base may repeat forever in another. One tenth is exact in decimal and infinitely repeating in binary, 0.0001100110011…, which is why 0.1 + 0.2 does not equal 0.3 in most programming languages. We come back to this when we reach IEEE-754.
