Revision · before the midterm Midterm review

A practice paper, and how to use it

Forty-three problems built to the shape of the midterm and overlapping none of it: nineteen multiple-choice questions and eight worked problems, one for each kind of question the paper asks, every one with a full solution hidden until you click.

Nothing on this page appears on the midterm, and nothing on it is taken from the exercise sets you have already worked, the slides or the quizzes. That is deliberate. A revision sheet made of problems you have seen measures your memory of the answers; this one measures whether you can do the thing.

How to use it

Work it with the solutions closed, on paper, in one 75-minute sitting, with no calculator — the same conditions as the exam. Then open the solutions. Reading a worked answer you have not attempted feels like learning and is not: it builds recognition, and the exam asks for production.

Printing the page — or saving it as a PDF — opens every solution and drops the navigation, which gives you a clean problem set with an answer key at the back.

The deck says outright that a Karnaugh map with don’t-cares will be on the midterm, and that you may be asked to draw a circuit diagram. Both are true. If you ration your revision time, those two get the first hour.

R.2The shape of the paper

Eight problems, 100 points, 75 minutes, closed book. No calculator, no notes, no phone. Problem 1 is the multiple choice and is worth 30 points; Problems 2–8 are worth 10 points each and take one topic apiece, in roughly the order of the table above.

You may leave two blank

Problems 1, 5 and 8 are required — the multiple choice, the Karnaugh map and the fixed/floating-point problem. Of the remaining five you may leave up to two blank.

Every problem you answer is counted, and then your highest-scoring problems are counted a second time — one extra for each problem you left blank. So Problems 2–8 always contribute 70 points however many you attempt:

  • leave none blank — all seven count once;
  • leave one blank — your best score is counted twice;
  • leave two blank — your best two scores are each counted twice.

A problem counts as blank if you write nothing, or if you cross out your work on it entirely.

What that means for how you sit the exam

The arithmetic is worth doing once, now, rather than under time pressure on the day. Suppose your two best worked problems earn 9 and 8.

  • Leave two blank. You hand in five scores — 9, 8 and three others — and the 9 and the 8 are each counted twice.
  • Attempt a sixth and score 2 on it. Now you hand in six scores and only the 9 is counted twice. You gained 2 points and lost the second count of the 8: a net loss of 6.

So the rule is the reverse of the usual exam advice: an extra attempt is worth making only if you expect to score more on it than on a problem you are already good at. If a problem is going badly and you still have a blank in hand, cross your work out entirely and spend the time checking the problems you can do.

A workable 75 minutes: 15 minutes on the multiple choice, then the two required problems while you are fresh — the K-map and the floating point are the longest two on the paper — then the optional ones in whatever order suits you, then five minutes at the end to check. If you are attempting all seven, you have about eight minutes each and will need to keep moving.

Show your work

From the syllabus, word for word: writing down an answer alone — even a correct one — receives no credit. On a K-map that means the circled groups. On a conversion it means the division chain. On a subtraction it means the negation step. An unsupported right answer and an unsupported wrong answer score the same.

R.3What to be able to do cold

Go down this list and mark the ones you could not do right now on blank paper without hesitating. Those are your revision list; the rest is reading you have already done.

Bases

Decimal to any base by repeated division. Any base to decimal by place value. Binary to octal and hexadecimal by grouping from the right, with no arithmetic. How many digits a value needs in a given base, without converting.

Boolean algebra

De Morgan in both directions, including over a bracket. The combining law AB + AB′ = A and its dual. Absorption, in both the plain form and the X + X′Y form. Enough fluency to simplify a three-term expression and name the law at each step.

Truth tables and canonical forms

Build a table one gate-column at a time. Read the canonical SOP off the 1-rows and the canonical POS off the 0-rows, getting the complementing the right way round. Move between an expression, a table, and Σ / Π shorthand in any direction.

Karnaugh maps

Lay out a four-variable map with the columns in Gray order. Circle maximal groups, including the ones that wrap round the edges and the four corners. Name the prime implicants, say which are essential, and justify a cover. Group the 0s for a POS. Use don’t-cares when they help and ignore them when they do not.

Circuits

Draw a circuit from an expression working inside out, with one inverter per complemented literal and dots on the fan-out junctions. Read an expression back off a diagram. Convert a two-level AND–OR circuit to NAND only, and count the gates both ways.

Arithmetic

Two’s complement in any width: represent, negate, read back. Subtract by adding the negation. Say what the carry out means, and detect signed overflow from the signs. The range a width covers and why it is lopsided.

Fixed point

Read Fixed⟨w,  b⟩ as a two’s complement integer scaled by 2−b, in both directions. The step size, the range, and which decimal values are representable at all.

IEEE 754

Encode: normalise, bias the exponent by 127, drop the hidden 1. Decode: split the fields, put the hidden 1 back, shift. The reserved patterns — zero, infinity, NaN — and why they are needed.

R.4Part A — multiple choice

Nineteen questions, one right answer each. On the paper there are fifteen of these and they are worth two points apiece, so they are the best-paid minute of the exam — and the easiest place to lose marks by rushing. Give yourself twenty minutes for the set below.

The solution to each one names the lecture it comes from, so a run of wrong answers in one area tells you what to re-read.

Morse is a variable-length code: E is one dit, Q is four elements. What lets a receiver tell where one letter ends and the next begins?(A) Every letter is padded out to the same number of elements.(B) The gaps are part of the code — one unit between elements of a letter, three between letters, seven between words.(C) Every letter ends with a dah.(D) The sender transmits the length of the letter first.

(B) The gaps are part of the code — one unit between elements of a letter, three between letters, seven between words.

This is the point of the whole lecture: the silence carries information. Strip the gaps out and ·− ··· and · −·· · become the same transmission. A variable-length code needs some way of marking boundaries, and Morse uses timing rather than a special symbol.

A lab has 300 machines to label, and the labels are to be hexadecimal numbers starting at 0. How many hexadecimal digits does each label need?(A) 4(B) It depends on how the numbers are assigned.(C) 2(D) 3

(D) 3

Two digits reach 0xFF = 255, and 255 < 299. Three digits reach 0xFFF = 4095, which is plenty. The general rule: d digits in base b cover bd values, so you need the smallest d with bd ≥ N.

The trap is answering 2 because 300 is a three-digit number in decimal. Digit counts do not carry across bases.

Write one fixed value in base 8 and again in base 16. The base-16 string is:(A) never longer(B) always the same length(C) shorter only when the value is a power of two(D) always shorter

(A) never longer

The digit count is ⌊logbN⌋ + 1, which falls as the base rises — but it falls in steps, so it often stays put. 7 is 78 and 0x7: one digit either way. 2907 is 55338 and 0xB5B: four digits against three.

"Always shorter" is the tempting answer and it is wrong for every value below 8.

A program sorts strings by comparing their ASCII bytes one at a time. It puts Zoo before apple. Why?(A) Byte sorting ignores case.(B) Shorter strings always sort first.(C) Every uppercase code is numerically smaller than every lowercase code.(D) Z and a happen to be adjacent in the table.

(C) Every uppercase code is numerically smaller than every lowercase code.

Uppercase runs 0x41–0x5A and lowercase runs 0x61–0x7A, so the whole uppercase block sits below the whole lowercase block. Z is 0x5A, a is 0x61.

They are not adjacent: six punctuation codes sit between them. That gap is the reason the case bit trick — set bit 5 for lowercase — works for letters and nothing else.

Of the sixteen rows of a four-variable truth table, how many have exactly two of the four inputs equal to 1?(A) 8(B) 12(C) 4(D) 6

(D) 6

Choose which two of the four are 1: that is 4C2 = 6. The rows are 3, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 12 — the ones whose binary form has exactly two 1s.

Worth knowing because row counts by weight are how you sanity-check a map: on a four-variable K-map those six cells are exactly the ones two steps from cell 0.

Three of these four expressions equal A. Which one does not?(A) A(A + B)(B) A + A′B(C) A · A(D) A + AB

(B) A + A′B

A + A′B = A + B, not A. The other three all collapse: A + AB = A(1 + B) = A and A(A + B) = A + AB = A, both by absorption, and A · A = A by idempotence.

The one to watch is the pair A + AB and A + A′B, which look alike and behave completely differently. A + AB throws the second term away; A + A′B keeps it and drops the A′. The reason is worth having in words rather than as a rule: in A + A′B the second term can only fire when A is 0, so it adds the rows where B is 1 and A is not — which is exactly what is missing from A.

Boolean algebra has no subtraction and no division. When you simplify an expression, the practical consequence is:(A) An expression can only shrink or stay the same — nothing is ever taken away to leave a negative term.(B) You may divide both sides of an identity by a common variable.(C) A + A must be written 2A.(D) Terms cancel in pairs, as they do in ordinary algebra.

(A) An expression can only shrink or stay the same — nothing is ever taken away to leave a negative term.

A + A = A, not 2A; A · A = A, not A². There is no inverse operation to undo + or ·, so a simplification step either absorbs a term into another or leaves things as they were. If your working ever produces a “−AB” you have left Boolean algebra.

This is also why you cannot cancel A from AB = AC to conclude B = C. Take A = 0: the equation holds for any B and C at all.

A ⊕ B ⊕ A equals:(A) 0(B) 1(C) B(D) B′

(C) B

XOR is associative and commutative, so regroup as (A ⊕ A) ⊕ B = 0 ⊕ B = B.

The two facts doing the work are A ⊕ A = 0 and 0 ⊕ B = B. Together they say every value XORed in twice disappears, which is the whole basis of parity checking: XOR a parity bit in, XOR it in again, and you are back where you started.

F(A, B, C) = Σ(0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7). F is:(A) A + B + C(B) ABC(C) the constant 1(D) the constant 0

(C) the constant 1

All eight rows are 1, so the output never depends on the inputs at all. The canonical SOP has eight product terms and 24 literals; the minimal SOP is the single symbol 1, and the circuit is a wire tied high.

A + B + C is wrong because it is 0 when A = B = C = 0, which is row 0 — and row 0 is in the list.

On a four-variable K-map over A, B, C, D, a group covering cells 0, 2, 8 and 10 gives the product term:(A) B′D′(B) A′C′(C) B′C′(D) A′B′

(A) B′D′

Write the four cells out: 0 = 0000, 2 = 0010, 8 = 1000, 10 = 1010. A takes both values and C takes both values, so both drop out. B is 0 throughout and D is 0 throughout, so they stay, complemented: B′D′.

These are the four corners of the map, and they are a legal group — the map wraps left to right and top to bottom. Four cells eliminate two variables, which is the check to run on any answer: a group of 2k cells always leaves 4 − k literals.

Two students minimise the same function with don’t-cares, use the X cells differently, and hand in different answers — both three terms and seven literals. This means:(A) The function has no minimal form.(B) They must have labelled the map’s axes differently.(C) One of them has made a mistake.(D) Both are correct: the minimum cost can be reached by more than one expression.

(D) Both are correct: the minimum cost can be reached by more than one expression.

Minimality is a property of the cost, not of the expression. When two covers tie, both are minimal and both earn full marks. It happens often with don’t-cares, because each X you are free to use or ignore doubles the number of maps you are really minimising.

What is not allowed is a different cost. If one answer is three terms and the other four, the four-term one is wrong, however carefully it was drawn.

A function has been minimised to a sum of three product terms. Built as a two-level NAND-only circuit, how many NAND gates does it need, not counting any used to make complemented inputs?(A) 3(B) 4(C) 6(D) 7

(B) 4

One NAND per product term, one NAND to collect them: 4, the same count as the AND–OR version. The conversion is free because the two bubbles added on each wire cancel — (XY)′ feeding a NAND that complements again gives back XY + ZW + ….

The gates you may still need are inverters for literals that appear complemented, and a NAND with both inputs tied together is the inverter.

Two 4-bit unsigned numbers are added. The result needs at most how many bits?(A) 4(B) 5(C) 8(D) 9

(B) 5

The largest case is 15 + 15 = 30, and 30 needs five bits (11110). In general adding two n-bit numbers gives at most n + 1 bits — the carry out of the top stage is that extra bit.

8 is the answer to a different question: that is what multiplying two 4-bit numbers needs.

Negate the 8-bit two’s complement pattern 10000000 by flipping every bit and adding 1. The result is:(A) 00000000(B) 10000001(C) 01111111(D) 10000000

(D) 10000000

Flip: 01111111. Add 1: 10000000 — the pattern you started with.

That pattern is −128, and +128 does not fit in eight bits. Two’s complement covers −128 to 127: one more negative value than positive, so the most negative number is the one value with no counterpart. It is the single case where negation overflows.

You add a positive and a negative number in two’s complement. The addition:(A) overflows exactly when the result is negative(B) always overflows(C) can never overflow(D) overflows exactly when the carry out is 1

(C) can never overflow

The sum lies between the two operands, and both of them already fit, so the answer fits too. Overflow needs two operands of the same sign and a result of the other sign.

The carry out is a red herring here: 46 − 25 produces a carry out of 1 and is perfectly correct, while 44 + 61 in seven bits produces a carry out of 0 and is wrong. The carry out tells you nothing about signed overflow.

An 8:1 multiplexer has its three select lines driven by A, B and C, and every data input tied to a constant 0 or 1. How many different functions of A, B and C can it produce?(A) 256(B) 512(C) 8(D) 64

(A) 256

Eight independent data inputs, two choices each: 28 = 256. That is every function of three variables there is — a truth table with eight rows has 28 possible output columns, and the data inputs are that column.

This is the clean way to see why a multiplexer is universal: wire the variables to the selects and copy the truth table into the data inputs.

In a 32-bit ALU built from 32 one-bit ALUs, signed overflow is detected:(A) at the most significant bit, from the carry into and the carry out of that one stage(B) by the Zero output(C) by the B-invert control line(D) inside every 1-bit ALU

(A) at the most significant bit, from the carry into and the carry out of that one stage

Overflow is the top stage’s business alone: it has occurred when the carry into bit 31 differs from the carry out of bit 31. Only that one ALU needs the extra gate, which is why the diagram in the textbook shows the top slice drawn differently from the other 31.

Zero is an OR over all 32 result bits, inverted — a different signal answering a different question.

The smallest positive value Fixed⟨12, 5⟩ can represent is:(A) 1/5(B) 1/32(C) 1/12(D) 1/4096

(B) 1/32

Five bits sit after the binary point, so the last one is worth 2−5 = 1/32, and that is the step between neighbouring values everywhere in the range. The step depends on b alone; the width w sets how far the range reaches, not how fine it is.

1/4096 would be the answer if all twelve bits were fractional.

How many IEEE 754 single-precision bit patterns represent zero?(A) none — zero is a special case handled outside the format(B) one(C) two(D) 223

(C) two

0 00000000 0…0 is +0 and 1 00000000 0…0 is −0. The format has a separate sign bit, so zero gets two encodings, and the standard requires them to compare equal.

Zero needs a reserved pattern at all because of the hidden 1: every ordinary number is 1.fff × 2e, which can never be zero. The all-zero exponent field is the escape hatch.

One problem for each of Problems 2–8 on the paper, plus a problem on adders, multiplexers and the ALU, which the paper tests through the multiple choice. These are longer than the exam versions on purpose: each one pushes a little past what you will be asked, so that the real question feels like the easy case.

Sixty minutes for all of them, on paper, solutions closed.

Where this goes wrong

Grouping from the left. 101101011011 has twelve bits so the two groupings happen to come out even here, but the habit will cost you the moment the bit count is not a multiple of three or four. Always pad on the left and group from the right.

9999 in base 6. No conversion needed — just find the first power of 6 that passes it. 64 = 1296 and 65 = 7776 and 66 = 46656, so 7776 ≤ 9999 < 46656 and the answer is six digits: the leading digit is the one worth 65.

The pattern

Powers of two have a shortcut between them because 8 = 2³ and 16 = 2⁴; base 6 has no such relationship with base 2, so there is no regrouping trick and division is the only way across. That is the real content of the question.

Simplify (A + B)(A + B′) and then (A + B)(A′ + B). Name the law you use at each step, and say what the pair of answers tells you.

First.

(A + B)(A + B′)
= AA + AB′ + AB + BB′   (distributive)
= A + AB′ + AB + 0   (idempotent AA = A; complement BB′ = 0)
= A(1 + B′ + B)   (distributive, backwards)
= A · 1 = A   (1 absorbs; identity)

Second. The same five steps with the roles swapped give B.

What the pair shows. In each product the variable that appears uncomplemented in both brackets is what survives. That is the dual of the combining law AB + AB′ = A: swap every + with · and every · with +, and a true identity turns into another true identity. Learning the laws in dual pairs halves the list.

One-line version

(A + B)(A + B′) = A + BB′ = A + 0 = A, using the distributive law in its Boolean-only form X + YZ = (X + Y)(X + Z). That form has no counterpart in ordinary algebra, which is why it is the one people forget.

Remove the outer complement from ((W + X)Y)′ using De Morgan’s laws, and simplify until no complement covers more than a single variable.

Outer De Morgan — the complement of a product is the sum of the complements:

((W + X)Y)′ = (W + X)′ + Y′

Inner De Morgan — the complement of a sum is the product of the complements:

(W + X)′ = W′X′

So ((W + X)Y)′ = W′X′ + Y′.

Check one row. W = 0, X = 1, Y = 1: the original is ((0+1)·1)′ = 1′ = 0; the answer is 1·0 + 0 = 0. Agreed. Checking a single well-chosen row catches most De Morgan slips in about ten seconds.

The classic error

Writing ((W + X)Y)′ = (W′ + X′)Y′. De Morgan does not just distribute the prime inwards — it also swaps every + with · and every · with +. Miss the swap and you have written down a different function.

Simplify A′BC′ + A′BC + ABC′, naming the law used at each step. Then confirm the answer with a Σ list.

Group the first two — they differ only in C:

A′BC′ + A′BC = A′B(C′ + C)   (distributive)
= A′B · 1 = A′B   (complement; identity)

That leaves A′B + ABC′. Factor B out:

A′B + ABC′ = B(A′ + AC′)   (distributive)
= B(A′ + C′)   (absorption with a complement: X + X′Y = X + Y)
= A′B + BC′   (distributive)

Confirm with Σ. The three starting terms are rows 2, 3 and 6, so F = Σ(2, 3, 6). The answer: A′B is rows 2 and 3, BC′ is rows 2 and 6 — together {2, 3, 6}. Same function, three terms down to two, nine literals down to four.

Why the overlap is fine

Row 2 is covered twice, by both terms. In Boolean algebra that costs nothing — 1 + 1 = 1 — which is exactly why overlapping groups on a K-map are allowed and often necessary.

(b) Shorthand. The 1-rows are 0, 2 and 6; the 0-rows are everything else.

F = Σ(0, 2, 6) = Π(1, 3, 4, 5, 7)

Both describe the same function. Σ lists where it is 1, Π lists where it is 0, and the two lists always partition 0–7.

(c) Canonical forms. One product per 1-row, complementing the variables that are 0:

F = A′B′C′ + A′BC′ + ABC′

One sum per 0-row, complementing the variables that are 1:

F = (A + B + C′)(A + B′ + C′)(A′ + B + C)(A′ + B + C′)(A′ + B′ + C′)

Rederive that flip rather than memorising it. A product fires when all its literals are 1, so to make it fire on row 010 you need A′, B, C′. A sum fails when all its literals are 0, so to make it fail on row 001 you need (A + B + C′).

(d) Minimal SOP. A′C′ covers rows 0 and 2; BC′ covers rows 2 and 6:

F = A′C′ + BC′

And here is the thing to notice: factor it and you get C′(A′ + B) — the expression you were handed. The circuit in the question was already minimal. Three canonical products of three literals each collapsed to two products of two, which is the whole argument for not building circuits straight from a truth table.

(e) The complement. F′ is 1 exactly where F is 0:

F′ = Σ(1, 3, 4, 5, 7)

Complementing is free in Σ notation — swap the list for its complement in 0–7. It is not free in algebra, where you would need De Morgan twice.

F(A, B, C, D) = Σ(0, 2, 8, 9, 10, 11, 15).
(a) Fill in the map.
(b) List every prime implicant, say which are essential, and justify each one in a sentence.
(c) Give the minimal sum of products.
(d) Now group the 0s instead and give the minimal product of sums.
(e) Which of the two circuits is cheaper, and by how many literals?

Prime implicants: B′D′, AB′, ACD.
Essential: all three of them.

Each one is forced. B′D′ is the only group covering cell 0; AB′ is the only group covering cell 9; ACD is the only group covering cell 15. Once every prime implicant is essential there is nothing left to choose and the answer is unique.

Minimal SOP: B′D′ + AB′ + ACD  — 3 terms, 7 literals

B′D′ is the four corners of the map. They are adjacent because the map wraps in both directions, and missing that group is the most expensive single mistake available on this question.

Minimal POS. Group the 0s — cells 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 12, 13, 14 — and complement each literal as you read the group off:

(A + D′)(B′ + D)(B′ + C)  — 3 terms, 6 literals

(e) Six literals against seven, so for this function the OR–AND circuit is cheaper — by one literal, which is one gate input. Neither form is cheaper in general; you only know which by minimising both, and that is why a question that asks for both usually wants the comparison said out loud.

F(A, B, C, D) = Σ(0, 6, 8, 9, 10, 13, 15) + d(2, 3, 5, 11).
Give the minimal sum of products and the minimal product of sums. Then say how much the don’t-cares were worth, by working out what the answer would have been with every X forced to 0.

Minimal SOP: B′D′ + A′CD′ + AD  — 3 terms, 7 literals

Cells 2 and 11 were taken into groups because they made the groups bigger — 2 turns the pair {0, 8} into the four corners, 11 lets AD be a block of four. Cells 3 and 5 were left alone because nothing they could join would have helped. You are never obliged to cover an X, and you are never penalised for covering one.

Minimal POS — group the 0s at 1, 4, 7, 12, 14, using the same X cells freely:

(A + D′)(B′ + C + D)(A′ + B′ + D)

This one has a tie: (A + D′)(A + B′ + C)(A′ + B′ + D) is also three terms and eight literals. Both are minimal and both are right.

What the X cells bought

Force every X to 0 and the same function minimises to

B′C′D′ + A′BCD′ + AB′D′ + AB′C′ + ABD  — 5 terms, 16 literals

against 3 terms and 7 literals. The don’t-cares more than halved the circuit. This is why the deck says they are free simplification if you use them — a student who treats every X as 0 loses nothing in correctness and everything in cost.

Draw the gate circuit for F = A(B′ + C) + A′C′, using two-input gates only. Count the gates, and say which input has the largest fan-out.

Work inside out: the inverters first, then the inner bracket, then the two products, then the final sum.

Gate count: three inverters (B′, A′, C′), one OR for the bracket, two ANDs for the two products, one OR to collect them — seven gates. The longest path is four gates deep, B → inverter → OR → AND → OR, and it is that depth rather than the gate count that sets how long the circuit takes to settle.

Fan-out. A drives the top AND and the inverter that makes A′; C drives the inner OR and the inverter that makes C′. Both have fan-out 2, and B has fan-out 1. Mark those junctions with a dot — a crossing without a dot is two wires passing, not a connection, and that convention is worth marks.

One inverter per literal

C′ is needed once and C is needed once, so one inverter does. If an expression used C′ in three places you would still draw one inverter and fan its output out three ways. Drawing a fresh inverter each time is a gate-count error, not a drawing style.

Reading order

Start at the inputs and label every wire as you go, writing the expression for each gate output on the drawing itself. People who try to read a circuit backwards from F almost always lose a complement somewhere in the middle.

F = A′B + BC′.
(a) Draw the two-level AND–OR circuit and count the gates.
(b) Convert it to a circuit using NAND gates only. Draw that and count again.
The symbol ↑ is used below for NAND: X ↑ Y means (XY)′.

(a) AND–OR. Two inverters, two ANDs, one OR — five gates. B feeds both ANDs, so it has fan-out 2.

(b) The conversion. Push a pair of bubbles onto every wire between the two levels. Two complements in a row cancel, so the function is untouched, but each AND-with-a-bubble is a NAND and the OR-with-bubbles-on-its-inputs is a NAND as well, by De Morgan:

F = A′B + BC′ = ((A′B)′ (BC′)′)′ = (A′ ↑ B) ↑ (B ↑ C′)

Count. Three NANDs for the two-level part, plus two more used as inverters to make A′ and C′ — five gates, the same as the AND–OR version. The two-level part converts one-for-one; only the literals that need complementing cost anything extra.

NAND as an inverter

Tie both inputs of a NAND together: A ↑ A = (AA)′ = A′. That one trick is why {NAND} on its own is functionally complete — with NOT and AND available you can reach everything.

As unsigned. 9 + 7 = 16, and 16 does not fit in four bits (0–15). The carry out is the missing fifth bit, and dropping it leaves 0. Wrong — and the carry out is exactly the flag that says so.

The same bits, two answers

Nothing about the adder changed. The hardware added two bit patterns; you decided what they meant. This is the single most important idea in the arithmetic unit: unsigned overflow is read off the carry out, signed overflow is read off the signs, and the circuit computes both flags and lets the program pick.

Why this works at all

A multiplexer is a one-hot selector: exactly one data input reaches the output, chosen by the selects. Put k of the variables on the selects and you have split the function into 2k smaller functions of the rest — and with one variable left over, those smaller functions can only be 0, 1, C or C′.

For the 1-bit ALU that computes AND, OR and add, give the control settings — B-invert, the carry in to the least significant bit, and the 2-bit operation code — for each of AND, OR, add and subtract. Then say why subtract is not an operation code of its own.

AND0000the AND gate’s output

subtract1110the adder’s Sum output — the same wire as add

Why subtract has no code of its own. A − B is A + (−B), and in two’s complement −B is B flipped plus 1. B-invert does the flipping; the carry in of 1 at the bottom stage does the adding of 1. So subtraction reuses the adder unchanged, and the multiplexer is still being told to pass Sum. One adder, two operations, no subtractor anywhere on the chip.

Note that B-invert has to reach every one of the 32 slices — all the bits of B need flipping — while the carry in of 1 goes to the bottom slice only.

Everything at once

The AND gate, the OR gate and the adder all run on every operation. The ALU does not decide what to compute and then compute it; it computes all three and throws two away. Selecting is one gate delay, and deciding first would cost more than it saved.

Write −37 and +37 in 8-bit two’s complement. State the range 8 bits covers and say why it is not symmetric.

+37 first: 32 + 4 + 1, so 00100101.

−37: flip every bit and add 1.

flip 00100101 → 11011010, add 1 → 11011011

Check. Add the two patterns: they should give zero with a carry out. 00100101 + 11011011 = 1 00000000 — eight zeros and a carry out of 1, which is discarded. Agreed.

Range. 8 bits give 256 patterns, covering −128 to +127. It is not symmetric because 00000000 is spent on zero: that leaves 255 patterns for the non-zero values, and the odd one out goes to the negative side. The consequence is that −128 has no positive counterpart, so negating it overflows.

A 7-bit machine is asked to evaluate 46 − 25. It has an adder and no subtractor.
(a) Write down the two bit patterns that actually arrive at the adder’s inputs.
(b) Do the addition and give the 7-bit result.
(c) The carry-out flag comes up 1. Should the programmer treat that as an error? Say exactly what it does mean.

+46 = 0101110   +25 = 0011001

(a) What the adder sees. The machine never subtracts; it negates and adds. Flip 25 → 1100110, add 1 → 1100111 = −25. So the adder’s two inputs are 0101110 and 1100111.

  0101110
+ 1100111
----------
 10010101     carry out 1, discarded

The low seven bits are 0010101 = 21, and 46 − 25 = 21. Agreed.

(c) The carry out is not an error. For signed arithmetic it carries no information at all, and here it is thrown away — which is not a fudge. Working in 7 bits is arithmetic modulo 128; adding −25 as the pattern for 103 gives an answer 128 too big, and dropping the eighth bit subtracts exactly 128 again. The two errors cancel by construction, which is the whole reason two’s complement is the representation everyone uses.

What the flag does mean is this: had the programmer meant these patterns as unsigned numbers, 46 + 103 = 149 would not have fit in seven bits, and the carry out is the warning. Signed or unsigned is the programmer’s decision, not the adder’s, so the hardware raises both flags and lets the program read whichever one applies. Here the signed reading is the intended one, 21 sits comfortably inside −64…63, and the carry out is ignored.

On the same 7-bit machine, 44 + 61 produces a result the programmer did not expect.
(a) Do the addition and give the 7-bit result.
(b) Say what went wrong, and why the carry-out flag did not catch it.
(c) Give a rule that does catch it, stated two ways: once in terms of the signs, and once in terms of the carries.

+44 = 0101100   +61 = 0111101

  0101100
+ 0111101
----------
 01101001     carry out 0

The result reads 1101001 = −23, and 44 + 61 = 105. Seven bits reach only 63, so 105 was never representable and the sum has wrapped round into the negatives.

Why the carry out missed it. It is 0 here, because nothing fell off the left-hand end: the unsigned sum 105 fits in seven bits perfectly well. The carry-out flag answers the unsigned question, and the unsigned answer is right. It is the signed reading that has broken.

The rule. Signed overflow has occurred when the two operands have the same sign and the result has the other one. Here 0… + 0… gave 1…, so overflow. Equivalently, and this is the version the hardware uses: overflow is the carry into the top bit XOR the carry out of it. Into bit 6 here the carry is 1 and out of it the carry is 0, so 1 ⊕ 0 = 1.

Carry out is not overflow

In part (b) the carry out was 1 and the answer was right. Here the carry out is 0 and the answer is wrong. The carry out answers the unsigned question; the sign rule answers the signed one. Confusing the two is the single most common error on this topic.

Here are two 7-bit patterns: 1010110 and 0101010.
(a) Which is larger if the machine is reading them as unsigned?
(b) Which is larger if it is reading them as two’s complement?
(c) Give both signed values, showing the step that tells you, and name the one bit that decided your answer to (b).

(a) Unsigned. Place values straight off: 1010110 = 64 + 16 + 4 + 2 = 86, and 0101010 = 32 + 8 + 2 = 42. The first is larger.

(c) The signed values. The second pattern starts with 0, so it is positive and reads the same way: +42. The first starts with 1, so it is negative; negate it to find the magnitude:

flip 1010110 → 0101001, add 1 → 0101010 = 32 + 8 + 2 = 42

So 1010110 = −42. The two patterns are negations of one another, which is why the same 42 turns up twice.

(b) Signed. −42 < 42, so now the second pattern is larger — the opposite of the unsigned answer. The bit that decided it is the leading bit: in a signed reading it carries weight −64 instead of +64, a swing of 128, and nothing else in the pattern can outweigh it.

A faster route to (c). Read the pattern as unsigned — 86 — then subtract 27 = 128: 86 − 128 = −42. Same answer, and it is really the definition: in w-bit two’s complement the top bit carries weight −2w−1 while every other bit carries its usual positive weight.

Why machines keep both

This is exactly why a processor has separate signed and unsigned comparison instructions. The bits do not say which you meant.

Read the single pattern 11001001 three ways:
(a) as an 8-bit two’s complement integer;
(b) as Fixed⟨8, 4⟩;
(c) as Fixed⟨8, 2⟩.
Then say in one sentence what the three answers have in common.

Fixed⟨8, b⟩ is eight bits with b of them after the binary point, and the whole thing is one two’s complement integer scaled by 2−b. So all three readings share a single piece of arithmetic.

Read the integer. The top bit is 1, so negate to find the magnitude: flip 11001001 → 00110110, add 1 → 00110111 = 55. So (a) the integer is −55.

Scale it. (b) −55 / 24 = −55 / 16 = −3.4375. (c) −55 / 22 = −55 / 4 = −13.75.

What they have in common: the same integer every time. The bits carry no record of where the binary point is — b is something you are told, not something you can read off, and changing it by one doubles or halves the value.

Check (b) the other way. Put the point in: 1100.1001. Negate the whole pattern to get 0011.0111 = 2 + 1 + ¼ + ⅛ + 1/16 = 3.4375, and the sign bit says it is negative. Agreed.

The trap

Reading 1100.1001 as “−something” by taking 100.1001 as the magnitude. The sign bit is not a separate flag here — the representation is two’s complement across all eight bits, so you must negate the whole pattern.

Encode −6.125 as Fixed⟨8, 3⟩. Give the eight bits, and state the step size and range of the format.

Scale first, then negate — that order keeps you out of trouble.

6.125 × 23 = 49

49 in eight bits is 00110001. Negate: flip → 11001110, add 1 → 11001111.

Check. Put the point three from the right: 11001.111. Negating gives 00110.001 = 6 + ⅛ = 6.125, negative. Agreed.

Step and range. The last bit is worth 2−3 = ⅛, so the step is 0.125 everywhere. The integer runs −128…127, so the value runs −16 to 15.875.

Why 6.125 is exact

6.125 = 6 + ⅛, and ⅛ is a power of two, so the binary expansion 110.001 terminates. A value like 6.1 would not terminate, would not fit any Fixed⟨w, b⟩, and would have to be rounded. Fixed point is exact for the dyadic fractions and for nothing else.

(a) Convert 45.75 to IEEE 754 single precision; give all 32 bits.
(b) How many of the 32 bits change if the value is negated? Write the pattern for −45.75.
(c) Again without redoing the work, write the encoding of 91.5.

(a) Magnitude in binary. 45 = 101101 and 0.75 = ½ + ¼ = .11, so 45.75 = 101101.11.

Normalise. Move the point five places left: 1.0110111 × 25.

Sign. Positive, so 0.

Exponent. 5 + 127 = 132 = 10000100.

Mantissa. The bits after the leading 1 — 0110111 — padded with zeros to 23.

0 10000100 0110 1110 0000 0000 0000 000

The leading 1 of 1.0110111 appears nowhere in the stored pattern. It does not need to: every normalised number has one, so the standard leaves it out and gets a 24th bit of precision for free.

(b) Exactly one bit changes. Flip the sign bit and leave the other 31 alone:

1 10000100 0110 1110 0000 0000 0000 000

Floating point is sign-and-magnitude, so negating is a single bit — unlike two’s complement, where you would flip everything and add 1.

(c) 91.5. 91.5 is exactly 2 × 45.75, so the significand is unchanged and the exponent goes up by one: 132 → 133 = 10000101.

0 10000101 0110 1110 0000 0000 0000 000

Multiplying by any power of two only ever moves the exponent field. That is the whole reason the format stores a binary exponent rather than a decimal one.

Two places to lose marks

Storing the leading 1 in the mantissa field, and forgetting the bias. The exponent field is never the exponent — it is the exponent plus 127, so that negative exponents can be stored without a second sign bit.

(a) Decode this single-precision pattern:

1 10000011 0010 1100 0000 0000 0000 000

(b) Then, without recomputing anything, write down the pattern for half that value.

Split the fields. Sign 1; exponent field 10000011; mantissa field 0010 1100 0000 0000 0000 000.

Sign. 1, so the number is negative.

Exponent. 10000011 = 131, and 131 − 127 = 4.

Significand. Put the hidden 1 back: 1.00101100… = 1 + 2−3 + 2−5 + 2−6 = 1 + 0.125 + 0.03125 + 0.015625 = 1.171875.

Put it together. −1.171875 × 24 = −18.75.

Check by going back. 18.75 = 10010.11 = 1.001011 × 24; exponent field 4 + 127 = 131 = 10000011; mantissa 001011 padded out. Agreed.

Shifting the binary point four places right is usually quicker than the arithmetic: 1.00101100 with the point moved four right is 10010.1100 = 16 + 2 + ½ + ¼ = 18.75.

(b) Half of it. Halving divides by 21, so the significand is untouched and the exponent drops by one: 131 → 130 = 10000010.

1 10000010 0010 1100 0000 0000 0000 000

That is −9.375, and only the exponent field moved. Halving a fixed-point number is a shift of every bit; halving a floating-point number is a subtraction of 1 from one field, which is why scaling is cheap in this format and why the exponent is stored separately in the first place.

A student stores 0.1 in a single-precision variable, adds it to itself ten times, and finds the result is not 1. Explain, in terms of the format, exactly what has happened. Would Fixed⟨32, 16⟩ do any better?

0.1 is not a dyadic fraction. In binary it is 0.0001100110011…, repeating for ever, exactly as 1/3 repeats in decimal. A format with a finite mantissa has to cut it off, so what gets stored is the nearest representable number — about 0.100000001490116119384765625, a touch too big.

Ten of those added up miss 1 by a little, and the intermediate rounding at each addition moves the answer again. The error is not a bug in the hardware; it is the exact arithmetic of the numbers that are actually in the registers.

Fixed point does not rescue it. Fixed⟨32, 16⟩ can represent exactly the multiples of 2−16, and 0.1 is not one of them either. Any binary format — fixed or floating, however wide — represents exactly the fractions whose denominators are powers of two, and 1/10 is not among them.

What does fix it

Work in integer cents rather than floating-point dollars, or use a decimal format whose base is 10. The lesson for this course is narrower and worth saying plainly: a floating-point value is an approximation with a known size of error, and comparing two of them with = is almost always the wrong thing to write.

R.6Ten things that cost marks

These are the errors that turn up again and again. None of them is a failure of understanding; all of them are cheap to stop making.

  1. Grouping bits from the left. Pad on the left, group from the right. 10110110 1 is not 0xB6?.
  2. Confusing the carry out with signed overflow. They are different flags answering different questions, and each is routinely 1 when the other is 0. The carry out is the unsigned story; the signs of the operands and the result are the signed one.
  3. Missing the wrap-around groups on a K-map. The left edge is adjacent to the right edge, the top to the bottom, and the four corners form a legal group of four. A map drawn without that in mind produces an answer that is correct and not minimal — which is the wrong answer to the question that was asked.
  4. A group of three cells. Groups are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 cells and nothing else. A group of three is not a product term; it is two groups or a mistake.
  5. Treating every don’t-care as 0. Legal, and it will usually cost you several terms. X cells are free to use. Equally: nobody makes you cover one.
  6. De Morgan without the swap. (X + Y)′ = X′Y′, not X′ + Y′. The prime moves in and the operator flips. If only one of those happened, you have changed the function.
  7. Drawing a new inverter every time a literal appears complemented. One inverter per literal, fanned out. Anything else inflates the gate count, and the gate count is usually part of the marks.
  8. Keeping the leading 1 in the mantissa field. It is hidden; that is the whole point. And the exponent field is never the exponent — it is the exponent plus 127.
  9. Reading a fixed-point pattern as sign-and-magnitude. Fixed⟨w, b⟩ is two’s complement across the whole width. To find the magnitude of a negative one you negate the entire pattern, not just the bits after the sign.
  10. Writing the answer and nothing else. It scores zero even when it is right. Division chains, circled groups, the negation step, the gate-by-gate columns — that is what is being marked.
