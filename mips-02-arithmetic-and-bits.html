Read an instruction as a state change

Most three-register arithmetic instructions use the form operation destination, source1, source2. For example, sub $t2,$t0,$t1 replaces $t2 with the old $t0 minus the old $t1. Operand order matters for subtraction.

A destination can also be a source: add $t0,$t0,$t1 reads the old values before writing the new $t0. That is useful for accumulation, but the previous $t0 is lost. Choose registers according to which values must remain available later. A register-role table is part of a program's design, not decoration.

Worked example: translate an expression

Translate f=(g+h)-(i+j). Let g,h,i,j occupy $s0,$s1,$s2,$s3 and let f use $s4. The dependencies require two sums followed by a subtraction.

add $t0, $s0, $s1
add $t1, $s2, $s3
sub $s4, $t0, $t1

For g=10,h=6,i=3,j=4, the states are $t0=16, then $t1=7, then $s4=9. Temporary registers represent intermediate expression values. Parentheses disappear because the instruction order already determines what happens first.

A different register assignment may also work. Its correctness depends on preserving needed sources and placing the final value in the required destination, not on matching one particular temporary name.

Constants inside instructions
An immediate is a constant encoded in an instruction. addi $t0,$t0,4 adds 4 without loading it into another register. The basic instruction has a signed 16-bit immediate, directly representing −32768 through 32767. That immediate is sign-extended to 32 bits before the addition.
li is a pseudo-instruction that loads a constant; the assembler chooses its expansion. A large constant can require multiple basic instructions. Immediate operands do not use brackets. Parentheses in a later memory operand such as 4($t0) have a separate addressing meaning.
The logical-immediate instructions andi, ori, and xori zero-extend their 16-bit immediate. Arithmetic offsets and bit masks therefore require different interpretations of their immediate fields.

Bitwise operations work one position at a time
Write 3 as 0011 and 5 as 0101. AND keeps a position only when both bits are 1; OR keeps it when either is 1; XOR keeps it when exactly one is 1. NOR complements the OR result across all 32 positions.
OperationResult
3 AND 51
3 OR 57
3 XOR 56
NOR of 3 and 50xfffffff8, interpreted as −8
NOT 30xfffffffc, interpreted as −4
Bitwise AND is not generally logical AND: 2 and 4 are both nonzero, but their bitwise AND is zero. Normalized Boolean flags containing only 0 or 1 can safely be combined with bitwise AND.
not $t2,$t0 is a pseudo-instruction. The basic equivalent nor $t2,$t0,$zero complements every bit. Complementing x gives −x−1 under signed two's-complement interpretation.

Shifts and a useful one-bit mask
sll shifts left, fills on the right with zeros, and loses bits that leave the register. For small values without lost significant bits, shifting by k multiplies by 2k. srl shifts right with zero fill; sra shifts right by copying the sign bit.
For −3, srl by one produces 2147483646; sra by one produces −2. Signed division −3/2 instead produces −1 because it truncates toward zero. Arithmetic right shift is therefore not an unconditional replacement for signed division.
andi $t1, $t0, 1
This mask extracts the low bit: 0 means even, 1 means odd. It works for negative two's-complement integers as well.

32-bit results have a finite range
Signed 32-bit integers range from −231 to 231−1. add, addi, and sub detect signed overflow. Adding 1 to 2147483647 cannot produce a valid signed 32-bit answer, so an overflow exception occurs.
addu, addiu, and subu keep the low 32 bits without raising signed-overflow exceptions. Their names do not make the registers unsigned. In particular, addiu still sign-extends its immediate. Whether wrapping is acceptable depends on the algorithm.
Choose example input ranges that keep every intermediate and final answer valid. A final answer fitting in 32 bits does not prove that each intermediate computation fits. Decimal and hexadecimal displays show the same stored bits under different presentations.

Worked example: a 64-bit product
li $t0, 3
li $t1, -5
mult $t0, $t1
mfhi $t2
mflo $t3
Two signed 32-bit inputs can require a 64-bit product. mult places the result in the pair HI:LO. mfhi copies the upper half; mflo copies the lower half. For 3×−5, HI:LO is 0xffffffff_fffffff1. The low half interpreted as signed is −15.
A negative fitting result has sign-extension ones in HI, so nonzero HI alone is not proof of signed overflow. A product fits signed 32 bits when HI equals the sign extension of LO. Retrieve any required result before another multiply or divide overwrites HI/LO.
Learn and work through
Worked example: quotient and remainder
li $t0, -7
li $t1, 3
div $t0, $t1
mflo $t2
mfhi $t3
With the two-register signed div, LO receives the quotient and HI receives the remainder. The quotient truncates toward zero, so −7/3 gives −2. The identity dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder gives −7=3×(−2)+(−1), so the remainder is −1.
Exclude division by zero and INT_MIN/−1 from these introductory examples. Copying LO or HI does not erase the special register. The supplied arithmetic lab saves its multiplication result before executing division; it prints 8, −15, −2, and −1.

Optional reading: R-format instruction bits
A basic instruction is 32 bits. R-format fields are opcode(6), rs(5), rt(5), rd(5), shift amount(5), and function(6). Five-bit register fields can name 32 registers. Assembly operand order is not identical to encoded field order.
For add $t0,$t1,$t2, rd=8, rs=9, rt=10, opcode=0, shift amount=0, and function=32:
000000 01001 01010 01000 00000 100000
0x012A4020
The destination is written first in assembly but occupies rd, not rs. Decode by splitting fields before interpreting their numbers.
Optional reading
Optional reading: I-format and alignment
I-format has opcode(6), rs(5), rt(5), and immediate(16). addi $t0,$t0,-1 uses opcode 8, rs=8, rt=8, and immediate 0xffff, producing 0x2108ffff. Sign extension turns the immediate into −1.
A MIPS word is four bytes. Ordinary word loads and stores require an address divisible by four. Width and alignment are distinct: four bytes can exist at an unaligned address even though an ordinary aligned word access cannot be used there. Memory addressing will become central in the arrays lesson.
The alignment rule is also explained in the required memory lesson (Lecture 6). The optional topic here is the detailed instruction encoding.
