Exercise set 8 · after the adder lecture Adding and subtracting

Arithmetic out of gates

The half adder and the full adder; chaining them into a ripple-carry adder; two’s complement as a way of writing negative numbers; and the payoff — subtraction with no subtracting hardware at all.

Everything in Sets 3 to 7 was about building some function from gates. This set builds one particular function that the rest of the machine is organised around. Addition is the fundamental arithmetic operation: subtraction can be simulated with it, multiplication is repeated addition, and division is repeated subtraction. Get addition into hardware and the rest follows.

The second half of the set is the trick that makes it pay. Representing negative numbers in two’s complement means a subtractor is not a new circuit — it is the adder you already built, with one small change on the way in.

8.1The half adder

Start with the smallest possible question: add two single bits. There are four cases, and the answer needs two bits of output, because 1 + 1 is 2, which is 10 in binary. Call the two outputs Sum and Carry out.

ABCarry outSum
0000
0101
1001
1110

Read the columns and both are familiar. Carry out is 1 only when both inputs are 1, which is AND. Sum is 1 when the inputs differ, which is XOR. So

Sum = A ⊕ B     Carry out = AB

Two gates. This is the half adder, and the name is a complaint: it adds two bits, but when you add multi-bit numbers each column also receives a carry from the column to its right, and this circuit has nowhere to put it.

8.2The full adder

The full adder fixes exactly that: three inputs — A, B and a carry in — and the same two outputs. Eight rows now:

ABCarry inCarry outSum
00000
00101
01001
01110
10001
10110

Both output columns have a clean description. Sum is 1 when an odd number of the three inputs is 1, which from Set 4 is the parity function:

Sum = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin

And Carry out is 1 when at least two of the three inputs are 1 — a carry is generated when the column total reaches two. As a sum of products that is

Carry out = AB + A·Cin + B·Cin

one term per pair. The circuit that is usually drawn uses a slightly different but equivalent form, because it lets the A ⊕ B already computed for the Sum be reused:

Five gates, and the signal A ⊕ B feeds two of them. Exercise 8.5 checks that the two expressions for Carry out really are the same function.

Two half adders make a full adder

There is a second way to build it that explains the names. Add A and B with one half adder; add its Sum to the carry in with a second; and OR the two carry outputs together, since a carry can be produced by either stage but never by both.

Exercise 8.6 asks you to verify that last claim, which is the only part of this construction that is not obvious.

8.3The ripple-carry adder

Now add n-bit numbers. Column by column, exactly as you would on paper: each column adds its two bits plus whatever carry arrived from the column to its right, and passes its own carry to the left. That is one full adder per bit, with the carry out of each wired to the carry in of the next:

This is the ripple-carry adder, and the name describes how it behaves in time. The carry has to travel from the rightmost stage to the leftmost one stage at a time, so the top bits of the answer are not correct until the carry has rippled all the way along.

Worked example

0110 + 0101 in four bits, which is 6 + 5:
carry in1000
A0110
B+ 0101
sum1011
carry out0

Only one column generates a carry here, the one holding 1 + 1, and the stage to its left receives it. The answer is 1011 = 11, and the carry out of the leftmost stage is 0, so the result fitted in four bits.

Note what the carry in to the rightmost stage is for ordinary addition: 0. Nothing has been carried into the lowest column because there is no column to its right. That input looks wasted — and §8.5 is about to use it.

8.4Negative numbers

The hardware above only knows how to add. Rather than design a subtractor, change how negative numbers are written so that subtracting becomes adding.

The idea is visible in decimal first. To work out 742 − 386 without borrowing, note that

A − B = A + (1000 − B) − 1000

and 1000 − B is easy: subtract each digit from 9 and add one. So 742 − 386 becomes 742 + 614 = 1356, and then throwing away the leading 1 does the −1000 for free, leaving 356. One addition and one digit crossed out, and no borrowing anywhere.

Binary works the same way with powers of two instead of powers of ten, and it is even easier: subtracting a bit from 1 is just flipping it. That gives the two’s complement procedure:

Writing a number in two’s complement
  1. Choose a width — a power of two larger than the magnitude you need. If |X| < 2k, then k + 1 bits is enough, and every number in the problem uses the same width.
  2. Write |X| in binary, padded with leading zeros to that width.
  3. If X is positive, stop. That is the answer.
  4. If X is negative, flip every bit and add 1.

The leading bit ends up acting as a sign: 0 for positive numbers and 1 for negative ones. It is not a separate sign flag, though — it is an ordinary bit of the number, and it carries weight like any other. That is why the arithmetic works out.

8.5Subtraction by addition

With negatives written this way, A − B is computed by writing −B in two’s complement and adding.

Worked example

27 − 11 in 6 bits.

Write both numbers. 27 = 011011 and 11 = 001011.

Negate the second. Flip every bit of 001011 to get 110100, then add 1: −11 = 110101.

Add.
carry in111110
27011011
−11+ 110101
sum010000
carry out1

The carry out of the leftmost column is 1, and it is discarded — that is the −2n in the identity above, happening automatically. What is left is 010000 = 16, which is 27 − 11.

So the subtractor is the adder. The only hardware a processor needs in order to subtract is a way to turn B into −B on the way in — flip the bits, and add one. Both halves of that are cheap, and the second half is free: remember the carry in to the rightmost stage that was sitting at 0. Set it to 1 and it is the “add one”.

That observation is the whole design of the subtract path in an ALU, which is where Set 9 goes.

8.6Range, and the discarded carry

A fixed width holds a fixed number of patterns, and in two’s complement they are split almost evenly between negative and non-negative:

The range is asymmetric: with 4 bits you can write −8 but not +8. Zero takes up one of the non-negative slots, so there is one more negative value than positive one. Exercise 8.13 is about the odd number this creates.

Two different “carries”

The carry out of the leftmost stage means different things depending on what you are doing, and confusing them is the usual source of trouble.

When you add two unsigned numbers, a carry out means the answer did not fit.

When you subtract using two’s complement, the carry out is the −2n term and is simply thrown away — it says nothing about whether the answer is correct.

Neither of those is the question “did this signed answer come out wrong?”, which is a third thing. Exercise 8.17 takes it up, and it is marked as going beyond what the lecture covered.

8.7Reference

  • Half adder. Sum = A ⊕ B, Carry out = AB. Two gates, no carry in.
  • Full adder. Sum = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin, Carry out = AB + A·Cin + B·Cin. Sum is odd parity; carry out is “at least two of three”.
  • Ripple-carry adder. n full adders, carry out to carry in. Carry in to the rightmost stage is 0 for addition.
  • Two’s complement. Positive: write it in binary. Negative: write the magnitude, flip every bit, add 1. Leading bit 1 means negative.
  • Negating. Flip and add one, in either direction.
  • Subtracting. A − B = A + (−B). Discard the carry out.
  • Range in n bits: −2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1.

8.8Exercises

Write the bits out. Every mistake in this topic is a bookkeeping mistake — a dropped carry, a width that changed halfway through, a flip that missed a bit — and they are all visible on paper and invisible in your head.

The adders

Build the truth table for adding two single bits, give Sum and Carry out as expressions, and explain what the word “half” is complaining about.
ABCarry outSum
0000
0101
1001
1110

Reading the two output columns against the gates of Sets 3 and 4:

Sum = A ⊕ B     Carry out = AB

Carry out is 1 only in the row where both inputs are 1, which is AND. Sum is 1 in the two rows where the inputs differ, which is XOR — and the row where both are 1 gives Sum = 0 with the 2 carried, exactly as 1 + 1 = 10 requires.

What “half” means. Adding two multi-bit numbers means adding a column at a time, and every column except the rightmost receives a carry from its neighbour. This circuit has two inputs and no way to accept that carry, so it can only ever be used for the rightmost column. It does half the job of a real adder stage — hence the name.

Worth noticing how small it is. The entire arithmetic of a processor is built from this, repeated and chained.

Draw the half adder as a gate circuit and count the gates. Then say what it costs if you are only allowed AND, OR and NOT.

Two gates, one XOR and one AND, with both inputs fanning out to both gates.

Without XOR, Exercise 4.1 built A ⊕ B from five gates — two inverters, two ANDs and an OR — so the half adder becomes six gates in all. The AND that produces the carry can be shared with nothing, because A′B and AB′ are the terms XOR needs and AB is not one of them.

This is a good example of why XOR earned its own symbol. A one-bit adder is two symbols on paper and six gates in silicon, and the symbol is the useful way to think about it.

Carry out is 1 in rows 3, 5, 6 and 7, so the canonical sum of products is

A′BCin + AB′Cin + ABCin′ + ABCin

On a map, with A down the side and B Cin across the top, those four cells form three overlapping pairs:

Carry out = AB + A·Cin + B·Cin

Three terms, each a pair of inputs, and the symmetry is the point: the carry does not care which two of the three inputs are 1, only that two of them are. Cell 7 — all three inputs 1 — is covered three times over, which costs nothing.

None of the three terms is redundant here, so this is minimal. Compare it with the consensus pattern of Exercise 4.12, where a third term was redundant: there the three terms were XY, X′Z and YZ, with one variable appearing in both polarities. Here all three literals are plain, and nothing drops out.

Show that the full adder’s Sum column equals A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin, and say why that is the expression you would expect.

The Sum column is 1 in rows 1, 2, 4 and 7 — inputs 001, 010, 100 and 111. Each of those has an odd number of 1s, and the rows with an even number (000, 011, 101, 110) all give 0.

From Exercise 4.6, a chain of XORs is 1 exactly when an odd number of its inputs are 1. So the Sum column is the three-input XOR:

Sum = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin

Why to expect it. Sum is the low bit of the total A + B + Cin, and the low bit of a total is its parity: odd totals end in 1, even totals in 0. The carry out holds the rest. So the two outputs are a clean division — Sum reports the parity and Carry out reports whether the total reached two.

Exercise 5.13 showed that this function’s canonical form has four minterms and no two of them combine, so as an AND–OR circuit the Sum output costs five gates. With XOR gates it costs two. The adder is the reason that gap matters.

Algebraically, expand the XOR and distribute:

Cin(A ⊕ B) = Cin(AB′ + A′B) = A B′ Cin + A′ B Cin

so the first form is AB + AB′Cin + A′BCin. The second form is AB + ACin + BCin, and expanding its last two terms over the missing variable gives ABCin + AB′Cin and ABCin + A′BCin. Both collect to minterms 3, 5, 6, 7 — the same function.

Why the circuit prefers it. A ⊕ B has already been built for the Sum output. Reusing that signal means the carry path costs one AND and one OR on top of it, rather than three fresh AND gates. Five gates in total rather than six.

Sharing a sub-expression between two outputs is the same move that saved a gate in the four-NAND XOR of Exercise 7.11, and it is the main thing hand optimisation of a multi-output circuit consists of.

A full adder can be built from two half adders and one OR gate. Explain why an OR is enough to combine the two carries — that is, show the two half adders can never both produce a carry.

The first half adder adds A and B, producing s1 = A ⊕ B and c1 = AB. The second adds s1 to Cin, producing the final Sum s1 ⊕ Cin = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin — which is right — and a second carry c2 = s1Cin.

They can never both be 1. Suppose c1 = 1. Then A and B are both 1, so s1 = A ⊕ B = 0, and therefore c2 = s1Cin = 0. The two carries are mutually exclusive, so OR-ing them loses nothing — there is never a case where OR would have to report “two carries” and cannot.

In arithmetic terms: the column total is at most 3, which is 11, so it needs only one carry bit. Two carries at once would mean a total of at least 4, which three single bits cannot reach.

The gate count comes out the same either way — two XORs, two ANDs and an OR — because a half adder is an XOR and an AND. The construction is worth knowing for the explanation it gives, not for any saving.

Ripple carry

Add 1001 and 0111 on a 4-bit ripple-carry adder. Show the carry into and out of every stage. Treating the inputs as unsigned, is the answer right?
carry in1110
A1001
B+ 0111
sum0000
carry out1

Stage by stage, from the right, with C0 = 0:

  • Bit 0: 1 + 1 + 0 = 2, so Sum 0, carry 1.
  • Bit 1: 0 + 1 + 1 = 2, so Sum 0, carry 1.
  • Bit 2: 0 + 1 + 1 = 2, so Sum 0, carry 1.
  • Bit 3: 1 + 0 + 1 = 2, so Sum 0, carry 1.

The four sum bits are 0000 and the carry out is 1.

Unsigned, the answer is wrong — or rather, incomplete. 1001 is 9 and 0111 is 7, so the total is 16, and 16 does not fit in four bits. The real answer is 10000, five bits wide; the adder kept the low four and handed you the fifth as the carry out. A carry out of 1 from an unsigned addition is exactly the signal “this did not fit”.

This is also the worst case for the ripple: every stage generated a carry, so the leftmost stage could not produce its output until the carry had travelled through all four. Exercise 8.8 puts a number on that.

A full adder takes 2 gate delays to produce its carry out once its inputs are stable. How long does a 32-bit ripple-carry adder take to produce a correct answer? Which output bit settles last, and why is this the adder’s central weakness?

The carry enters stage 0 at time 0. Stage 0’s carry out is ready at 2 delays, stage 1’s at 4, and so on: stage k’s carry out is ready at 2(k + 1) delays.

For 32 bits the last carry, out of stage 31, is ready after 64 gate delays, and the top sum bit needs a further delay or two after its carry in arrives. The answer is not trustworthy until then.

The last bit to settle is the most significant one, because it is at the far end of the chain. Every other stage is waiting on its right-hand neighbour.

Why it matters. The delay grows linearly with the width. Doubling from 32 to 64 bits doubles the time, and since a processor cannot start the next operation until this one is finished, that delay sets a ceiling on the clock speed. Everything else in a one-bit stage is fast; it is the carry chain, and only the carry chain, that is slow.

Note what is not the problem: gate count. A 32-bit ripple adder is 32 identical small circuits, which is cheap and easy to lay out. It is time, not area, that forces the issue — which is the subject of Exercise 8.18.

What is wired to the carry in of the rightmost stage when the adder is used for ordinary addition? What would the circuit compute if you set that input to 1 instead?

For ordinary addition it is tied to 0. The rightmost column has no column to its right, so nothing can have been carried into it.

Setting it to 1 computes A + B + 1. The 1 enters the lowest column and propagates upward like any other carry, so the whole result is one larger.

That looks like a strange thing to want until you put it beside the two’s complement rule from §8.4. Negating B means flipping every bit of B and adding 1. The flip can be done with one inverter per bit; the “add 1” needs an adder — except that the adder already has an unused input sitting at 0 that does exactly this.

So the complete subtractor is: invert every bit of B, set the carry in to 1, and the existing adder produces A − B. No second circuit, and the cost of the subtract capability is one inverter per bit plus a wire. Set 9 builds precisely this.

It is worth appreciating how fortunate this is. The carry in exists because stages in the middle of the chain need one; the rightmost stage inherits it for free; and that free input turns out to be exactly the piece two’s complement was missing.

Two’s complement

Write the 4-bit two’s complement representation of +5, −5, −1 and −8.

+5. Positive, so write it in binary and stop: 0101.

−5. Start from 0101, flip every bit to get 1010, then add 1: 1011.

−1. From 0001, flip to 1110, add 1: 1111. All ones is always −1, whatever the width — worth memorising, because it comes up constantly.

−8. From 1000 — which is 8 in plain binary — flip to 0111 and add 1, giving 1000, back where you started. That is not an error: see Exercise 8.13.

Check each with the place values, remembering that in two’s complement the leading bit carries weight −8 rather than +8: 1011 is −8 + 2 + 1 = −5, and 1111 is −8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = −1. That reading — the top bit is negative, the rest are normal — is the fastest way to convert back, and it is Exercise 8.11.

These are 8-bit two’s complement patterns. What decimal numbers are they? 00101100, 11111001, 10000000.

The leading bit tells you the sign immediately, and the quickest route for a negative one is to read the top bit as a negative weight.

00101100 starts with 0, so it is positive and reads as ordinary binary: 32 + 8 + 4 = 44.

11111001 starts with 1, so it is negative. Weighting the top bit −128 and the rest normally: −128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 1 = −7.

The other route is to negate and read: flip 11111001 to 00000110, add 1 to get 00000111 = 7, so the original was −7. Both work; the first is fewer steps.

10000000 is −128, the most negative 8-bit value. Negating it gives itself back, which is the special case of Exercise 8.13.

A sanity check worth doing every time: a pattern of mostly 1s is a small negative number, not a large one. 11111001 being −7 rather than −249 surprises people, and the surprise goes away once you think of the leading 1s as “no extra magnitude, just sign”.

What range of values does n-bit two’s complement cover? Why is the range asymmetric, and what are the ranges for 8, 16 and 32 bits?

n bits give 2n patterns. Those with a leading 0 are the non-negative values and those with a leading 1 are the negatives, splitting the patterns exactly in half:

−2n−1 … 2n−1 − 1

width nmost negativemost positivehow many values
4-8716
6-323164
8-128127256
16-327683276765536
32-214748364821474836474294967296

Why asymmetric. Zero has to live somewhere, and its pattern (all 0s) has a leading 0, so it is counted among the non-negatives. That leaves 2n−1 patterns for the negatives but only 2n−1 − 1 for the positives. One more negative value than positive.

This is the practical consequence of two’s complement having exactly one representation for zero — which is the property that makes the arithmetic work. Representations that keep the range symmetric, such as sign-and-magnitude, pay for it with two different patterns for zero and with addition hardware that has to test signs. The asymmetry is the price of the adder being simple, and it is a bargain.

The familiar numbers follow: an 8-bit byte holds −128 to 127, a 16-bit value −32,768 to 32,767, and a 32-bit word about ±2.1 billion. The last of those is why a signed 32-bit counter of seconds runs out in 2038.

Negate −6 in 4 bits and check you get +6 back. Then try to negate −8 in 4 bits. What happens, and why?

−6 in 4 bits is 1010. Flip: 0101. Add 1: 0110, which is +6. Negation undoes itself, as it must.

−8 is 1000. Flip: 0111. Add 1: 1000 — the same pattern, still −8.

Why. Negating should produce +8, and +8 is not representable in 4 bits: the range stops at +7. The hardware cannot report a number it has no pattern for, so it returns the only thing it can, which is the input. The asymmetry of Exercise 8.12 has to show up somewhere, and this is where.

The same holds at every width: −128 in 8 bits and −2,147,483,648 in 32 bits are their own negatives. It is a genuine edge case in real software — taking the absolute value of the most negative integer gives a negative answer, and in C that is undefined behaviour rather than a wrong number.

A useful way to see it: negation pairs each value with another, and there are an even number of patterns. Zero pairs with itself, so one other value must also pair with itself for the count to work out, and that value is the most negative one.

Here is a shortcut for negating: copy the bits from the right up to and including the rightmost 1, then flip everything to the left of it. Check it against flip-and-add-one on 01011000, and explain why it works.

The shortcut on 01011000: the rightmost 1 is in position 3, so copy that bit and the zeros to its right unchanged, and flip the rest. That gives 10101000.

The long way: flip 01011000 to 10100111, then add 1, which gives 10101000. Same answer.

Why it works. Look at what adding 1 does to the flipped number. The original ends in the rightmost 1 followed by some zeros; flipping turns that tail into a 0 followed by some ones. Adding 1 to a string of ones makes them all 0 and carries, and that carry stops at the first 0 it meets — which is exactly the flipped copy of the original’s rightmost 1, turning it back into a 1.

So the carry ripples through precisely the tail and dies there. Everything to the left of the rightmost 1 is flipped and never touched by the carry; everything from the rightmost 1 rightward ends up back where it started.

This is the method to use by hand, and it is also why negation is fast in hardware: one inverter per bit and a carry that, despite appearances, usually dies within a bit or two.

Subtraction

Compute 21 − 13 and 13 − 21 in 6-bit two’s complement. Show the negation and the addition for each, and state what happens to the carry out.

21 − 13. In 6 bits, 21 = 010101 and 13 = 001101. Negate the second: flip to 110010, add 1, giving −13 = 110011. Add:

The carry out is 1 and is discarded. The result 001000 has a leading 0, so it is positive, and it reads as 8.

carry in011110

Two things to take from the pair. The same hardware handled both, with no test of which number was larger — that is the whole point of the representation. And the carry out differed between them (1 against 0) while both answers were correct, which is Exercise 8.16.

In the subtraction above, the carry out of the leftmost column is thrown away. Why is that legitimate rather than losing information?

Work through what the hardware actually computed. Writing −B in n-bit two’s complement means writing the pattern for 2n − B. So adding gives

A + (2n − B) = (A − B) + 2n

which is the answer you wanted plus one extra 2n. And 2n in binary is a 1 followed by n zeros — a bit sitting one place to the left of everything the adder keeps. Discarding the carry out subtracts that 2n back off, which is the “ − 1000 ” from the decimal version in §8.4 happening by itself.

Why no information is lost. That bit is not part of the answer; it is an artefact of how −B was written. The n bits the adder keeps are the complete result, assuming it fits in n bits.

Which is also why the carry out says nothing useful here. In Exercise 8.15 the first subtraction produced a carry and the second did not, and both answers were right. For a two’s complement subtraction the carry out is noise — it reflects the borrowing pattern, not the correctness of the result. Reading it as an error flag is a common and costly mistake.

Compare Exercise 8.7, where the inputs were unsigned and a carry out did mean the answer had not fitted. Same wire, opposite meaning, and only the interpretation of the input bits decides which.

Not covered in class

This exercise goes past what the adder lecture covered, which stopped at the carry being discarded. It is here because the question comes up the moment you use the circuit, not because it is examinable.

5 is 0101 and 6 is 0110. Adding:
carry in1000
50101
6+ 0110
sum1011
carry out0

The four-bit result is 1011, which as a two’s complement number is -5 — but 5 + 6 is 11. Two positive numbers have added to give a negative one.

What went wrong. 11 is outside the 4-bit range, which stops at +7. The correct answer needs a bit that does not exist, and the sum has spilled into the position that holds the sign.

The rule. Two numbers of the same sign that produce a result of the opposite sign have overflowed. Adding numbers of opposite signs can never overflow, since the result lies between them in magnitude and both already fit.

In hardware this is detected without comparing signs at all: overflow has occurred exactly when the carry into the leftmost stage differs from the carry out of it. Check it here — the carry into the top stage is 1 and the carry out is 0, and they differ. A single XOR gate on two wires the adder already has.

Note how different this is from the carry out on its own. The carry out means “did not fit” for unsigned addition, means nothing for two’s complement subtraction, and is half of the overflow test for signed addition. Processors compute both flags on every operation and let the instruction decide which to look at.

Challenge. Exercise 8.8 showed a 32-bit ripple-carry adder takes 64 gate delays. Work out what the carry into stage k depends on, and explain how that lets a faster adder be built.

Where the time goes. The ripple adder is slow for one reason: stage k cannot start until stage k − 1 has finished. But that is a property of the circuit, not of the problem. The carry into stage k is a function of the original inputs A0…Ak−1 and B0…Bk−1, all of which are available at time zero. Nothing forces it to be computed one stage at a time.

Two signals per stage. Look at when a stage sends a carry leftward:

  • It generates a carry when A and B are both 1, regardless of what arrives from the right. Call that gk = AkBk.
  • It propagates a carry — passes one along if it receives one — when exactly one of A and B is 1. Call that pk = Ak ⊕ Bk.

Both are computable immediately from the inputs, in one gate delay, for every stage at once. And the carry out of stage k is

ck+1 = gk + pkck

which is the Carry out expression from §8.2 written in these terms. Expanding it down to the inputs:

c2 = g1 + p1g0 + p1p0c0

— a sum of products of signals that are all ready at once, so it can be computed in two more gate delays rather than waiting for the ripple. Every carry has such an expression, and in principle every one of them can be computed in parallel.

What that buys. A carry-lookahead adder computes the carries this way and gets the whole addition down to a handful of gate delays rather than 2n. The catch is in the expansion: the formula for ck has k + 1 terms and the longest needs a gate with k + 1 inputs, so a full 32-bit lookahead is unbuildable. Real designs use lookahead in blocks — four bits at a time, say, with the blocks themselves connected by a second level of the same trick — which gives a delay growing like log n instead of n.

Worth knowing

The trade is area for time, and it is the same trade as the balanced AND tree back in Exercise 3.8: a chain of n − 1 gates is as cheap as it gets and as slow as it gets, and spending more gates to shorten the longest path is the standard move. The adder is just the most valuable place to make it, since almost every instruction a processor executes waits on one.

Where this goes next

You now have a circuit that adds, and a representation that turns subtraction into addition. What a processor actually contains is one unit that does both of those plus the logical operations, with a few control wires choosing between them on each instruction. Building that — and the selector circuit that makes the choosing possible — is the next set.
