Exercise set 3 · after the gates lecture Gates and circuits

Drawing circuits, filling tables

The three gates AND, OR and NOT; turning a Boolean expression into a gate diagram and a gate diagram back into an expression; and building the truth table of a circuit one column at a time.

A gate is a little device that controls the flow of binary information. That is the whole idea, and everything in this set follows from it. Three gates are enough to build any digital circuit whatsoever — every adder, every register, every processor in the building — so it is worth being completely fluent with them before we add anything else. Fluency here means three separate skills, and this set drills all three: reading a gate, drawing a circuit from an expression, and deciding what a circuit actually computes by tabulating it.

Notation

Three notations for the same three operations appear in the course, the textbook and the exercises below, and you need to recognise all of them.

The AND dot is usually dropped, exactly as the multiplication sign is dropped in ordinary algebra: AB means A · B. This page keeps the dot in the statements of the exercises so that nothing is ambiguous, and drops it in the solutions once you are used to it.

3.1The three gates

Each gate has one or more input wires and one output wire. A wire carries a 0 or a 1 — physically, the absence or the presence of a voltage, or a switch open or closed — and the gate decides its output from its inputs. There is no memory and no timing here: change the inputs and the output follows immediately. That is what makes these circuits combinational.

AND — A · B
1 only when both inputs are 1.

OR — A + B
1 when at least one input is 1.

NOT — A′
flips its single input. The little circle is the inversion.

Committed to memory, the three gates are three short tables:

Two shapes worth learning by sight, because you will read them off a diagram hundreds of times this semester. The AND gate has a flat back and a round nose, like a capital D. The OR gate has a curved back and comes to a point. Students who confuse the two lose marks on every exam question in this unit, and the fix is entirely mechanical: flat back, AND.

The circle is the whole NOT

Inversion is drawn as a small circle, and the circle can sit on the output of any gate rather than on a triangle of its own. A small circle on an AND output therefore means "AND, then invert" — not "invert, then AND". Those are different functions, as Exercise 3.12 makes painfully clear, so read the position of the circle carefully.

3.2From an expression to a circuit

Drawing a circuit is not a creative act. There is a procedure, it always works, and following it slowly beats guessing at a layout.

  1. Find the last operation. Which operation would you perform last if you were evaluating the expression by hand? That gate is the output gate, and it goes on the right.
  2. Work backwards. Each operand of that operation is itself an expression; draw a gate for each one to its left. Repeat until every branch ends at a plain input variable.
  3. Draw a NOT for every prime. A primed variable is a NOT gate fed by that variable. Nothing more.
  4. Fan out where a variable is reused. A variable that appears in two places is one input wire that splits. Mark the split with a solid dot so a reader can tell a junction from two wires that merely cross.

The order of operations is the same as in ordinary algebra, with AND playing the role of multiplication: NOT binds tightest, then AND, then OR. So A + B · C means A + (B · C), and if you want the other grouping you must write the parentheses.

Worked example

Draw F = (A + B′) · C.

The outermost operation is the AND between the bracket and C, so an AND gate is the output gate. Its first operand is A + B′, which needs an OR gate; the OR gate's second operand is B′, which needs a NOT. Its other operand, A, is a plain input, and so is C. Notice that the three inputs enter on the left in a fixed order and the single output leaves on the right. Keeping to that habit makes your diagrams readable and, more practically, makes them easy to check against a truth table.

Without drawing anything: how many gates does F = A · B′ · C′ need, if every gate has at most two inputs?

Four. Two NOT gates, for B′ and C′, and two AND gates, because a two-input AND can only combine two things at a time: first A · B′, then that result with C′. Exercise 3.8 is about exactly this chaining.

3.3From a circuit to an expression

The reverse direction is easier, and it is the better way to check a drawing. Label the output of every gate with the expression it produces, starting from the inputs and moving right. Each label is built from the labels feeding into it. When you reach the last gate, its label is the expression for the circuit.

One warning, and it is the single most common error in this unit:

This circuit ANDs A and B and then inverts. Is its output A′ · B′?

No. Following the labels: the AND gate produces A · B, and the NOT gate inverts that, so the output is (A · B)′. That is a different function from A′ · B′, and one line of the truth table proves it: with A = 1, B = 0 the circuit gives (1·0)′ = 1, while A′·B′ gives 0·1 = 0.

The correct rewriting of (A · B)′ is A′ + B′ — the AND becomes an OR when the inversion moves inward. That is De Morgan's law, and Exercise 3.12 asks you to discover it from a table rather than take it on trust.

3.4Building a truth table

A truth table is the complete specification of a combinational circuit: one row per possible input combination, and the output for each. With n inputs there are 2n rows — 4 for two inputs, 8 for three, 16 for four. The rows are always listed in counting order, reading the input columns as a binary number, so that two people's tables for the same circuit can be compared line by line.

Do not try to evaluate the whole expression in your head, row by row. Give every intermediate signal its own column and fill the table column by column, left to right. Each column depends only on columns already written, so each one is a single-gate decision, and when an answer comes out wrong you can see which column went wrong.

Worked example

Tabulate the worked circuit above, F = (A + B′) · C. Three inputs, so eight rows; two intermediate columns, one per gate.

ABCB′A + B′F
000110
001111
010000
011000
100110

110010
111011

Read the shaded column from the top: 01000101. That eight-bit string is the function — two circuits are the same circuit, however differently they are drawn, exactly when their output columns match.

One sanity check before you trust a table: an AND output column can only have a 1 where both of its input columns have a 1, and an OR output column can only have a 0 where both have 0. Scan for a violation and you will catch most arithmetic slips in a few seconds.

3.5Conventions and reference

  • Inputs enter on the left, in alphabetical order, top to bottom. The output leaves on the right.
  • A solid dot is a junction — one signal going two places. No dot means the wires merely cross and are unrelated. Nothing about a diagram is sloppier, or more likely to cost you marks, than an ambiguous crossing.
  • Every gate output goes somewhere. A gate whose output is not used is not part of the circuit.
  • Fan-out is free on paper: one variable can feed as many gates as you like. Fan-in is not — unless a question says otherwise, assume AND and OR gates take exactly two inputs, and chain them when you need more.
Checking your own work

Drawing and tabulating are two independent skills, which means each can check the other. After drawing a circuit, label every gate output and read the expression back out; you should recover what you started from. After tabulating, test one or two rows by substituting the values into the original expression directly. Two independent routes to the same answer is as close to certainty as you get without a simulator — and from next week Logisim will be the simulator.

3.6Exercises

Pencil, paper and a ruler. The circuit diagrams here are drawn by machine, but the ones you draw by hand only need to be clear: straight wires, unambiguous junctions, and every gate the right shape.

Reading the gates

Give the output of each gate for each input combination: an AND gate, an OR gate, and an AND gate whose second input has been inverted first (that is, A · B′). Inputs AB = 00, 01, 10, 11.
ABA · BA + BA · B′
00000
01010
10011
11110

The last column is the interesting one. Inverting an input before it reaches an AND gate produces a gate that is 1 in exactly one case — A = 1, B = 0 — and that "exactly one row" behaviour is how every circuit later in the course is built: one AND gate per row of the table you want, then an OR gate to collect them.

Compare the OR column with the last one and notice they share no pattern at all. An inverter placed on an input changes which combination the gate responds to; it does not change the gate.

A single variable A is wired to both inputs of an AND gate, but one of the two connections passes through a NOT gate first. Draw the circuit and give its output for A = 0 and A = 1. Then answer the same question with the AND replaced by an OR.

With the AND gate, the output is A · A′. When A = 0 the inputs are 0 and 1, so the AND gives 0; when A = 1 they are 1 and 0, so the AND gives 0 again. The output is constantly 0, whatever A does. A and A′ can never both be 1, so an AND can never fire.

With an OR gate the output is A + A′, and by the same reasoning one of the two inputs is always 1, so the output is constantly 1.

These are the complement laws, A · A′ = 0 and A + A′ = 1. They are worth recognising on sight, because a circuit that contains one has gates in it doing no work at all — and stripping those gates out is the beginning of simplification.

Note the dot on the A wire. Without it the drawing claims A enters the AND gate and some unrelated signal enters the NOT, which is a different circuit.

Drawing circuits from expressions

Draw the circuit for F = X · Y′ + Z.

Last operation: the OR. Its operands are X · Y′, which needs an AND, and Z, which is a plain input. The AND's second operand Y′ needs a NOT. Three gates.

Read it back to check: the NOT gives Y′, the AND gives X · Y′, the OR gives X · Y′ + Z. That is what was asked for.

The commonest wrong answer puts the NOT gate on the output of the AND, which draws (X · Y)′ + Z instead. The prime in the expression is attached to Y alone, so the inverter belongs on the Y wire, before the AND gate.

Draw the circuit for F = (P + Q) · R′. Then say what changes if the parentheses are removed.

The parentheses force the OR to happen first, so the AND is the output gate.

Without the parentheses, P + Q · R′ means P + (Q · R′), because AND binds tighter than OR. The same three gates appear, but the AND and OR swap places: the AND now combines Q with R′ and the OR collects P with the AND's output. The two circuits are genuinely different functions — with P = 1, R = 1 the first gives (1+Q)·0 = 0 and the second gives 1 + (Q·0) = 1.

Where a drawing is ambiguous the parentheses are the thing to check first.

Draw two circuits: F = A′ · B′ and G = A′ + B′. How many gates does each need, and what is the only difference between the drawings?

Three gates each — two inverters and one two-input gate — and the only difference is the shape of that last gate.

The inverters sit on the inputs in both cases, because in both expressions the primes are attached to the variables individually. Neither drawing has a circle on the output of the final gate.

These two functions look almost identical on paper and behave quite differently: F is 1 only when A and B are both 0, while G is 1 unless they are both 1. Exercise 3.12 pairs each of them with a circuit that inverts at the end instead, which is where De Morgan's laws come from.

Draw the circuit for F = (A + B) · (A + C). Mark the fan-out correctly.

Two OR gates feeding one AND gate. A appears in both brackets, so the A input wire splits — one wire, one dot, two destinations.

Three gates, no inverters, and three input wires of which one fans out. If your drawing has two separate wires both labelled A entering from the left, redraw it: a variable is a single signal, and the dot is how a diagram says so.

This expression turns out to equal A + B · C, which needs only two gates. Exercise 3.11 asks you to verify that with a table, and it is the first real instance of something the rest of the unit is about: the same function has many circuits, and some are cheaper than others.

Draw the circuit for F = W · X + Y · Z′ + W′. Assume every gate has at most two inputs.

Three terms to be ORed together, and a two-input OR gate takes only two at a time, so two OR gates chain: first the sum of two terms, then that result with the third.

Six gates in total — two ANDs for the product terms, two NOTs for Z′ and W′, and two ORs to collect three terms. W is used twice, once at the first AND and once at its own inverter, so it fans out.

Any grouping of the three terms gives the same function: OR does not care about the order or the bracketing, so (W·X + Y·Z′) + W′ and W·X + (Y·Z′ + W′) are the same circuit redrawn. Choose whichever grouping keeps your wires from crossing.

A closing observation, for later: this function simplifies drastically. W′ already covers every case where W = 0, and W·X covers the W = 1 cases where X = 1, so the whole thing reduces to W′ + X + Y·Z′. Draw both and count the gates.

You have only two-input AND gates. Draw a circuit whose output is 1 exactly when A, B and C are all 1. Then generalise: how many two-input AND gates are needed for n inputs?

Chain them. AND A with B, then AND that result with C.

This works because AND is associative: (A · B) · C = A · (B · C), so the bracketing is yours to choose. Either chaining, or the balanced version that ANDs A with B and C with D before combining, computes the same function.

For n inputs you need n − 1 gates. Each gate consumes two signals and produces one, so it reduces the count of "things still to be combined" by exactly one; starting at n and finishing at 1 takes n − 1 steps, whatever shape you chain them in.

The shape does matter for one thing, though not for the count. A straight chain of n − 1 gates makes a signal pass through n − 1 gates in sequence; pairing them up in a balanced tree makes it pass through about log2n. Gates take time, so that difference is the whole subject of the carry-lookahead lecture later in the semester.

Read the last two columns down: 00101010 against 00101111. They differ in two rows, PQR = 101 and PQR = 111 — the two rows where P = 1 and R = 1.

The reason is structural, not arithmetic. In the bracketed version R′ gates everything: if R = 1 then R′ = 0 and the final AND forces F to 0 no matter what P and Q do. In the unbracketed version P reaches the output directly through the OR, so P = 1 forces the output to 1 regardless of R. Same three gates, opposite behaviour.

This is the exercise to remember when you are tempted to leave out a pair of parentheses.

The two output columns are identical, 00011111, so yes — the same function, computed two ways. This is the distributive law of Boolean algebra, A + B·C = (A + B)·(A + C), and it is the one law that has no analogue in ordinary arithmetic: 2 + 3×4 is not (2+3)×(2+4).

Build G. Both use three gates as drawn, but G's are two gates if you count only what is needed — one AND and one OR — against three for F, and G's longest path from an input to the output runs through two gates rather than two ORs plus an AND. Fewer gates is less silicon, less power and less delay.

Notice also what the shared column tells you: F is 1 whenever A is 1, and otherwise only when B and C are both 1. That sentence is a complete description of the function and it is much easier to hold in your head than either expression.

In one table, put columns for A′ · B′, (A + B)′, A′ + B′ and (A · B)′. Which pairs are equal? State the rule you have found.

Two pairs match, and they are not the pairs the notation suggests:

A′ · B′ = (A + B)′   and   A′ + B′ = (A · B)′

These are De Morgan's laws. In words: moving an inversion through a gate flips the gate. Invert the output of an OR and you get the AND of the inverted inputs; invert the output of an AND and you get the OR of the inverted inputs.

What is not true is the tempting distribution, (A + B)′ = A′ + B′. The table refutes it in the rows AB = 01 and 10, where the left side is 0 and the right side is 1. Priming is not multiplication and does not distribute over anything.

In circuit terms: a circle can be dragged from the output of a gate back onto both of its inputs, provided you change the gate's shape at the same time. That single move is what makes NAND and NOR gates so powerful, and it is where the functional-completeness lecture starts.

Reading circuits

Write down the Boolean expression computed by this circuit. How many gates would a two-input-only implementation of your expression need?

Label every gate output, left to right:

  • the upper AND gives A · B;
  • the inverter on A gives A′, and the OR below it gives A′ + B;
  • the inverter on C gives C′;
  • the second AND combines those two: (A′ + B) · C′;
  • the output OR collects both terms.

F = A · B + (A′ + B) · C′

Six gates, which is exactly what the diagram shows — two inverters, two ANDs and two ORs — so the drawing is already a two-input implementation and nothing needs adding.

Both A and B fan out, which is what the two dots on the left mark. Follow each dot with a finger before you write anything down: mistaking a junction for a crossing is the one error that makes an otherwise perfect reading wrong.

Give the expression for this circuit, build its truth table, and then find the simplest circuit with the same output column.

The OR gate gives A + B and the AND gate combines that with A, so

F = A · (A + B)

ABA + BF = A · (A + B)

The output column is 0011 — identical to the A column. So A · (A + B) = A, and the simplest circuit with this behaviour is a plain wire from A to the output. No gates at all, and B is not connected to anything.

This is one of the absorption laws, and the intuition is worth more than the name. For the AND to output 1, A must be 1 — and once A is 1 the OR gate is 1 too, whatever B does. So B can never affect the answer. Its partner law is A + A · B = A, by the same argument read the other way round.

Finding an input that cannot affect the output is always worth two seconds of attention before you start drawing. Here it removes the entire circuit.

Reasoning about circuits

Challenge. A circuit is built from AND and OR gates only — any number of them, wired any way you like, with no inverters anywhere. Show that its output must be 0 when all of its inputs are 0. What does that tell you about which functions AND and OR can build?

Work forward from the inputs. Set every input to 0 and consider the gates in an order where each gate comes after the gates feeding it — always possible in a combinational circuit, because there are no loops.

The claim is that every wire in the circuit carries 0, and it follows gate by gate. Take any gate and suppose every wire feeding it carries 0. If it is an AND gate, it needs both inputs at 1 to output 1, so it outputs 0. If it is an OR gate, it needs at least one input at 1, so it outputs 0 as well. Either way its output wire carries 0, and the supposition holds for the next gate along. The input wires carry 0 by assumption, so the property propagates from the inputs to the output, and the output is 0.

The consequence is a genuine limit, not a matter of cleverness. Any function whose value is 1 on the all-zeros input — the NOT of a variable, the constant 1, A′ · B′, (A + B)′ — is impossible to build from AND and OR gates alone, however many you use. Inversion is not a convenience; it is an independent capability, and that is why the lecture introduced three gates rather than two.

One step further

The same argument, run with every input at 1, shows an inverter-free circuit must output 1 when all inputs are 1. Between them the two facts say that such circuits are monotone: turning an input from 0 to 1 can never turn the output from 1 to 0. Monotone functions are a real and much-studied subclass, and the practical version of the statement is the one to carry forward — a circuit that must ever say "no because this input is on" needs an inverter somewhere upstream.

Where this goes next

Everything here has been mechanical: an expression in, a drawing or a table out. The two questions that make the subject interesting come next. Given a truth table, what is the cheapest circuit that produces it — that is Boolean algebra and K-maps. And what is the smallest set of gate types you need at all — that is functional completeness, where it turns out one gate type suffices for everything, including the adder the lecture set out to build.
