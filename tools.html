Reference Appendix B

Textbooks, tools and links

The books the course is built on — two of them free, legal downloads that between them cover the whole syllabus — followed by a short, opinionated list of web tools. Every entry says what it is good for, and, where it matters, what it will quietly do to your learning if you lean on it.

B.1Textbooks

Four books stand behind this course. You are not required to buy any of them — the lectures are self-contained — but each covers part of the material more slowly and more thoroughly than any set of notes can. Two of the four are free, legal, open-access downloads, and between them they cover both halves of our syllabus. Start there.

The hardware half

David A. Patterson and John L. Hennessy, Computer Organization and Design: The Hardware/Software Interface, Morgan Kaufmann. The MIPS edition.

The standard text for this subject, and the one the MIPS material in every computer-organization course descends from. Chapters 2 and 3 and Appendix B are the ones that overlap our syllabus: instructions, arithmetic, and the logic-design appendix. Dense, but the worked examples are excellent and the "Fallacies and Pitfalls" section at the end of each chapter is worth reading on its own.

Getting hold of it without paying full price. Any earlier edition is fine and used copies are cheap — nothing we cover in CSCI 240 changed between editions. And if you would rather not buy anything at all, the two free books below cover our syllabus between them.

A word about the PDFs you will find

Searching for this title turns up sites offering the whole book as a free PDF. Those are unauthorised copies, not free editions — the book is in copyright and its authors and publisher have not released it. Use the library, a used copy, or the open-access books below.

The logic half — free

Charles W. Kann, Digital Circuit Projects: An Overview of Digital Circuits Through Implementing Integrated Circuits, Gettysburg College, 2014. Open access.

Gates, Boolean algebra, truth tables, adders, multiplexers, flip-flops — built up as circuits you actually construct rather than diagrams you look at. It is written around Logisim, the same simulator we use, so the exercises drop straight into what you already have installed from Appendix A.

  • PDF from the author's college repository — the official free copy
  • Open Textbook Library entry — with reviews from other instructors
The assembly half — free

Charles W. Kann, Introduction to MIPS Assembly Language Programming, Gettysburg College, 2015. Open access.

Written for exactly the course you are taking, assumes only a first programming course, and moves at a sane pace. If Patterson and Hennessy feels like being handed a reference manual, start here instead. It uses MARS throughout, which is the simulator we use.

  • PDF from the author's college repository — the official free copy
  • Read it online at LibreTexts — the same book as browsable web pages, easier to search
A third opinion

Robert L. Britton, MIPS Assembly Language Programming, Pearson / Prentice Hall, 2004.

Short, blunt and example-driven. Useful when a MIPS idea has not clicked and you want to see the same thing explained by someone else in three pages rather than thirty.

On the software the older syllabus points at

You may see the previous syllabus link to Carl Burch's original Logisim and to the MARS home page. MARS is unchanged and is the one we use — see §B.6 below. Logisim is not: install Logisim-Evolution instead, following Appendix A. The original has been discontinued since 2014.

B.2How to use a converter without hurting yourself

An online base converter is a wonderful thing and a terrible study aid. The exam is on paper. If your practice consists of typing numbers into a website, you will arrive at the exam able to do exactly nothing.

The rule

Convert first, check second. Do the problem on paper, commit to an answer, and only then type it into the tool. Used that way a converter is the best possible study partner: instant feedback, unlimited problems, no waiting for office hours. Used the other way round it is a very efficient way to feel productive while learning nothing.

The random-bit generator in §B.2 exists precisely so you can generate your own problems and check your own work — a complete practice loop with no textbook needed.

B.3Numbers and bases

RapidTables number converter

Converts integers between binary, octal, decimal and hexadecimal, in any direction. The plainest of the converters and the one to use for checking Exercise set 2.

Note: it does not do quaternary or arbitrary bases. For those, convert to binary here and regroup by hand — which is the method from §2.4 anyway.

Random binary generator

Produces strings of random bits, as many and as long as you ask for. Use it to manufacture your own conversion practice: generate eight bits, convert to decimal and hexadecimal on paper, then check with the converter above.

Binary fraction converter

Handles the part after the radix point, in both directions. You will want this when we reach fixed-point numbers — and it is the quickest way to see for yourself that one tenth never terminates in binary.

WolframAlpha

For the odd bases nothing else covers. Type 2026 in base 12 or convert 462 base 7 to decimal and it answers, with the working. Useful for Exercises 2.10 and 2.11.

B.4Codes and characters

Morse Code World translator

Translates text to Morse and back, and plays it as audio at a speed you set in words per minute. That second feature is the one that matters for Exercise set 1: set it to 20 wpm, send a short message, time it with a stopwatch, and compare against the unit count you worked out on paper.

It also offers Farnsworth timing, which stretches the gaps while keeping the characters at full speed — the thing mentioned in the "Going further" box of Set 1.

ASCII table

All 128 codes with their decimal, hexadecimal, octal and binary values side by side, plus the control characters. The layout is dense but it is the one to check against when you are converting a character by hand.

Worth noticing while you are there: 'A' is 0x41 and 'a' is 0x61, differing in exactly one bit. That is not a coincidence, and we will come back to it.

B.5Logic and circuits

Logisim-Evolution

The simulator we use. Downloads are on the releases page; installation instructions for every platform are in Appendix A. The repository's wiki and docs folder hold the reference manual.

32x8 Karnaugh map solver

Enter a truth table for up to six variables and it returns the minimal sum-of-products and product-of-sums, and draws the circuit. Excellent for checking a K-map you have already drawn.

Warning, and it is a real one: K-map questions on the exam ask you to show the grouping. A solver gives you an answer with no groups drawn, and the answer is often written in a different but equivalent form from yours. Draw the map first.

B.6MIPS and assembly

MARS — MIPS Assembler and Runtime Simulator

The simulator for the second half of the course: assemble, run, single-step, and watch the registers and memory change as each instruction executes. Written for teaching, and it shows — the register display alone is worth the download.

MARS is a Java program, so it needs a Java runtime, the same one described in §A.6 of the Logisim appendix. A maintained build is also available from one of the original authors.

Eclipse Temurin (Java)

Where to get Java when a tool needs it. Take version 21 or newer, and the JDK rather than the JRE. Needed for MARS, and for the .jar route to Logisim-Evolution.

B.7Floating point

These come into their own around the IEEE-754 material. Bookmark them now; you will not remember they exist when you need them.

IEEE-754 converter

Type a decimal number and watch it decompose into sign, exponent and mantissa, with every individual bit clickable. Flip a bit and the value updates. Nothing explains the hidden bit and the exponent bias faster than a minute of clicking here.

Exploring Binary converter

Shows the exact decimal value your float actually holds, which is almost never the number you typed. This is the tool that makes the rounding-error discussion concrete rather than theoretical.
