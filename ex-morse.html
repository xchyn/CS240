Encoding, decoding and timing

Reading and writing the code; the dit–dah timing rules that make it decodable; words per minute and the PARIS standard; and the question that turns a code into engineering — how long does the message actually take?

Morse is the first variable-length code you meet in this course, and it is worth more attention than its age suggests. A fixed-length code — five bits per letter, say — is trivial to decode: chop the stream into five-bit pieces. Morse gives up that convenience deliberately, spends one unit on E and thirteen on Q, and buys back transmission time because English uses E far more often than Q. Everything in this set follows from that trade.

Reading the table

A dit (·) is the short element, a dah (−) the long one. Say them aloud as di and dah and the rhythm sticks faster than the picture does: C is dah-di-dah-dit, and you will never write it as −··− by mistake again.

Four punctuation marks come up often enough to be worth knowing: period ·−·−·−, comma −−··−−, question mark ··−−·· and slash −··−·. None of them appear in the exercises below.

1.2Timing: everything in units

Morse has exactly two symbols but five durations, and the three silent ones do the real work. Without them the stream would be ambiguous: ··· is S, but it is also EEE, and EI, and IE. The gaps are what separate them.

dahdown3 units
gap between elements inside a characterup1 unit

The mistake everyone makes once

Do not count the gap after the last character of a message. The gaps sit between things: a word of n characters has n − 1 character gaps, and a message of m words has m − 1 word gaps. The one place a trailing gap is counted is the PARIS standard in §1.3, and it says so explicitly.

1.3Speed: words per minute

A unit is a relative duration, not a time. To turn units into seconds you need one number: how long a unit lasts. Operators do not quote that number in milliseconds; they quote a speed in words per minute. To make "word" mean something exact, the convention picks a reference word:

The PARIS standard

The word PARIS plus the word gap that follows it is exactly 50 units:

PARIS4 character gaps1 word gaptotal
1157354×3 = 12750

So at W words per minute the key sends 50W units per minute, and one unit lasts

unit = 60 / (50W) second = 1200 / W milliseconds

1.5Exercises

Exercises 1–4 are warm-ups on the code itself, 5–12 are timing, 13–15 ask you to think about why the code is shaped the way it is. Assume International Morse and the standard timing throughout.

Reading and writing the code

Decode this message. Wider gaps separate characters; the slash is a word gap.
····  ·  −··−

···· is H, · is E, −··− is X. The message is HEX.

If you read the last character as −·· followed by − you got D then T — which is exactly the ambiguity the character gap exists to prevent. Count the gaps, not just the elements.

Encode BIT in Morse, and write out the full on/off sequence including every gap.

B is −···, I is ··, T is −:

−··· ·· −

Written as durations, using on for key down and off for silence:

on 3, off 1, on 1, off 1, on 1, off 1, on 1  (B, 9 units) ··· off 3  (character gap) ··· on 1, off 1, on 1  (I, 3 units) ··· off 3  (character gap) ··· on 3  (T, 3 units)

Total: 9 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 21 units.

Which letter is shortest, and which letters are longest? Do the digits follow the same pattern?

The shortest is E at 1 unit — a single dit — followed by T at 3. The longest letters are J (·−−−), Q (−−·−) and Y (−·−−), all 13 units.

The digits do not follow the pattern: every digit is exactly five elements, so they range from 9 units (5, five dits) to 19 units (0, five dahs). They are a fixed-length code embedded inside a variable-length one. That is a design decision, not an accident — digits carry no frequency advantage worth exploiting, and a uniform five-element block is easier to recognise in a call sign or a number group.

Encode CUNY and count its length in units.

C = 11, U = 7, N = 5, Y = 13, so the characters total 36 units. It is one word of four characters, so there are three character gaps: 3 × 3 = 9.

36 + 9 = 45 units

Verify the PARIS standard yourself: show that PARIS plus its trailing word gap is 50 units, and derive the formula for the unit duration at W words per minute.

P = ·−−·: 1+3+3+1 = 8 for the elements, plus 3 internal gaps, = 11.
A = ·−: 1+3 = 4, plus 1 gap, = 5.
R = ·−·: 1+3+1 = 5, plus 2 gaps, = 7.
I = ··: 1+1 = 2, plus 1 gap, = 3.
S = ···: 1+1+1 = 3, plus 2 gaps, = 5.

Characters: 11 + 5 + 7 + 3 + 5 = 31. Four character gaps: 12. Word gap: 7. Total 50.

If the operator sends W of these per minute, that is 50W units in 60 seconds, so one unit lasts 60 / (50W) seconds — that is 1.2 / W seconds, or 1200 / W milliseconds.

The trailing word gap belongs in the count precisely because words come one after another: sending PARIS twice takes 100 units, not 93.

How long does SOS take at 20 wpm? At 5 wpm?

From §1.2 the message is 27 units.

At 20 wpm the unit is 60 ms: 27 × 60 = 1620 ms = 1.62 s.

At 5 wpm the unit is 1200 / 5 = 240 ms: 27 × 240 = 6480 ms = 6.48 s, exactly four times as long. Halving the speed doubles every duration; the unit count never changes.

How long does CUNY take at 20 wpm?

45 units from Exercise 1.4, and 60 ms per unit:

45 × 60 ms = 2700 ms = 2.70 s

How long does HELLO WORLD take at 15 wpm?

Units. HELLO: H = 7, E = 1, L = 9, L = 9, O = 11, so 37 for the characters plus 4 × 3 = 12 for the gaps, giving 49.
WORLD: W = 9, O = 11, R = 7, L = 9, D = 7, so 43 plus 12, giving 55.
One word gap: 7.

49 + 55 + 7 = 111 units

Time. At 15 wpm the unit is 1200 / 15 = 80 ms, so 111 × 80 = 8880 ms = 8.88 s.

Sanity check: 111 units is a little over two PARIS-lengths, and at 15 wpm a PARIS-length takes 4 seconds. Two of them is 8 seconds. Close to 8.88 — good.

A message of 123 units took 11.35 seconds to send. What speed was the operator working at, in words per minute?

The unit duration is the total time divided by the unit count:

11350 ms / 123 = 92.3 ms per unit

and W = 1200 / unit-in-ms:

1200 / 92.3 = 13.0 → 13 wpm

(The message was QUEENS COLLEGE, if you want to check the 123 for yourself.)

At 20 wpm, how many units are sent per minute, and roughly how many characters?

By definition of the standard, 20 wpm means 20 × 50 = 1000 units per minute — which is another way of saying the unit is 60 ms.

For characters, PARIS is counted as a five-character word, so the nominal rate is 20 × 5 = 100 characters per minute. Real text differs: from §1.4, MORSE CODE is 89 units for 10 characters, or 8.9 units each, against PARIS's 50 / 5 = 10 units each. That text would run at about 1000 / 8.9 ≈ 112 characters per minute. Average English text sits close to the PARIS figure, which is why the standard was chosen.

Your instructor sends CS240 at 10 wpm. How long does it take, and why is it so much slower than a five-letter word?

Units. C = 11, S = 5, 2 = 15, 4 = 11, 0 = 19. Characters total 61, plus 4 × 3 = 12 for the gaps:

73 units

Time. At 10 wpm the unit is 120 ms, so 73 × 120 = 8760 ms = 8.76 s.

Why. PARIS is 43 units without its trailing gap; CS240 is 73. The three digits cost 45 units between them, because every digit is five elements regardless of how common it is. Digits are expensive in Morse, and that is the price of the fixed-length block described in Exercise 1.3.

Why the code looks like this

Compare the five most common letters in English (E, T, A, O, I) with five of the rarest (J, Q, X, Z, V). Add up the units in each group. What does the comparison tell you about how Morse was designed?

Common: E = 1, T = 3, A = 5, O = 11, I = 3 → 23 units.
Rare: J = 13, Q = 13, X = 11, Z = 11, V = 9 → 57 units.

The rare group costs about 2.5× as much as the common one. That is the whole idea: Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail are said to have set the code lengths by counting the type in a printer's case, which is a rough survey of English letter frequency. Assign the cheap codes to the letters you send most and the average message gets shorter, even though the worst case gets longer.

This is the same idea you will meet later as Huffman coding, done by hand a century early. Morse is not quite optimal — O at 11 units is expensive for a common letter — but it is close, and it was built without any theory of information to justify it.

Suppose you replaced Morse with a fixed-length code: 5 bits per character, one bit sent per unit of time. How many units would HELLO WORLD take, and how does that compare with Morse? If the fixed-length code wins, why was Morse used?

Five bits gives 25 = 32 codes, enough for 26 letters plus a space and a few controls. HELLO WORLD is 11 characters counting the space, so 11 × 5 = 55 units — against Morse's 111 from Exercise 1.9. The fixed-length code is about twice as fast.

So why Morse? Three reasons, and they are all about the receiver rather than the code:

  • No clock. Decoding a fixed-length stream requires both ends to agree on where each 5-bit block begins and to stay in step for the whole message. Morse re-synchronises at every gap, so a human ear — or a sloppy operator — never loses the thread.
  • No equipment. Morse is decoded by a person listening. A fixed-length binary code needs a machine at the far end, which in 1844 did not exist.
  • Error tolerance. Miscount one bit in the fixed-length stream and every character after it is garbage. Mishear one dit in Morse and you lose one letter.

The comparison is a genuinely modern one: it is the same trade-off between raw rate and self-synchronisation that shows up in every serial protocol you will meet later.

Challenge. An operator sends three dits with no gaps at all between them — just ··· as a continuous run. How many different messages could that be? Now do the same for four dits.

The letters made only of dits are E (1), I (2), S (3) and H (4). Without gaps, the question is: in how many ways can you split a run of n dits into pieces of size 1, 2, 3 or 4?

For three dits, the splits are

splitreads as
3S
2 + 1I E
1 + 2E I
1 + 1 + 1E E E

4 possibilities.

For four dits: 4 (H); 3+1 (SE); 1+3 (ES); 2+2 (II); 2+1+1 (IEE); 1+2+1 (EIE); 1+1+2 (EEI); 1+1+1+1 (EEEE) — 8 possibilities.

The counts double each time, at least while n ≤ 4: writing f(n) for the number of splits, f(n) = f(n−1) + f(n−2) + f(n−3) + f(n−4), because the first piece can be any of the four lengths. From f(0) = 1 that gives 1, 1, 2, 4, 8, 15, 29, 56, … — growing roughly geometrically.

The point: Morse without its gaps is not a decodable code at all. No prefix of the dit-dash string tells you where a character ends, so the silence is not padding — it is a third symbol doing essential work. Codes that need no such separator, where no codeword is a prefix of another, are called prefix-free, and that is exactly what a Huffman code gives you.

Going further

Two threads worth pulling if this interested you. First, Farnsworth timing: when teaching Morse the characters are sent at full speed but the gaps are stretched, so a beginner hears the correct rhythm of each letter while still getting time to think. The unit count no longer predicts the duration, because there are now two different unit lengths in one message. Second, the question of how good Morse actually is: weight each letter by its English frequency, compute the average units per letter, and compare with what a Huffman code built from the same frequencies would achieve. Morse comes out within a few percent — not bad for a code designed by weighing type in a print shop.
