Exercise set 6 · after the Karnaugh map lecture Karnaugh maps

Minimising by looking

The truth table redrawn so that combinable terms sit next to each other; grouping the 1s for a minimal sum of products and the 0s for a minimal product of sums; prime and essential prime implicants; and don’t-cares, which are free simplification if you use them.

Set 5 gave you a circuit for any truth table and no way to tell whether it was wasteful. Set 4 gave you the laws to shrink it, but spotting where a law applies in a list of product terms is a hunt, and there is no way to know when to stop. The Karnaugh map fixes both problems at once by redrawing the truth table so that terms which can combine are physically adjacent. Simplification stops being algebra and becomes looking at a picture.

The method is exact, not a heuristic: done properly it produces a genuinely minimal two-level expression. Its limit is human eyesight, which is why it runs out at four variables — and what happens past that is Exercise 6.21.

6.1The table, rearranged

A three-variable map has eight cells for the table's eight rows, and a four-variable map has sixteen. The cells hold the output column; the whole trick is in the order of the labels along the edges.

Read the column headings: 00, 01, 11, 10 — not 00, 01, 10, 11. This is Gray code, in which each label differs from its neighbour in exactly one bit. That single property is what the whole method rests on, because of the combining law from Set 4:

XY + XY′ = X

Two terms merge when they agree everywhere except one variable. On the map, two cells that touch horizontally or vertically differ in exactly one variable — that is what Gray ordering buys — so adjacent cells are exactly the pairs that combine. Finding simplifications becomes finding neighbours.

The edges wrap

Adjacency does not stop at the border. The left and right columns differ in one variable too (00 and 10 differ only in the first bit), so they are neighbours; the same goes for the top and bottom rows of a four-variable map. Think of the map as rolled into a cylinder both ways — a torus — rather than as a flat sheet. The four corners of a four-variable map are all mutually adjacent, which Exercise 6.7 puts to work.

Diagonal cells are never adjacent. They differ in two variables, and nothing combines them.

Filling a map is copying the output column into the right boxes, and the cell numbers above are the row numbers from Set 5 — so a function given as Σm(…) can be written straight onto the map without a table at all.

6.2Grouping

Circle the 1s in blocks, and read a product term off each block. The rules:

  1. Every group is a rectangle of 1, 2, 4, 8… cells — a power of two, and a full rectangle, wrapping around edges if it helps. Six cells in an L shape is not a group.
  2. Make every group as large as possible. Each doubling in size removes one literal from the term, so a group of four beats two groups of two.
  3. Cover every 1 at least once. Groups may overlap freely — X + X = X, so covering a cell twice costs nothing.
  4. Use as few groups as possible, because each group is one AND gate.

To read a term off a group, ask which variables are the same in every cell of it. Those go into the product, plain if they are 1 and complemented if they are 0. The variables that change across the group are the ones that dropped out.

Worked example

F(A, B, C) = Σm(0, 1, 3, 7), the running example for this page.

Two groups, each a pair:

  • Cells 0 and 1 sit side by side in the A = 0 row, in columns 00 and 01. Across them A is always 0 and B is always 0, while C changes. So the term is A′B′.
  • Cells 3 and 7 sit one above the other in column 11. Across them B and C are always 1 while A changes, giving BC.

F = A′B′ + BC

Two AND gates and an OR, against four 3-input ANDs and a 4-input OR for the canonical form — and reading it off took about ten seconds.

Notice what was not done. Cells 1 and 3 are also adjacent, giving A′C, and that is a perfectly legal group. It is also unnecessary: both of its cells are already covered. Adding it would add a gate and change nothing, and rule 4 says leave it out. Knowing which groups to leave out is what §6.3 is about.

6.3Prime and essential

Two pieces of vocabulary, because the exam uses them and because they turn "circle the biggest blobs" into a procedure that is actually reliable.

An implicant is any legal group — any product term that only ever makes the function 1. A prime implicant is one that cannot be made any bigger: no doubling of it stays inside the 1s. Only prime implicants can appear in a minimal expression, since a non-prime group is a term with an extra literal that could have been dropped.

An essential prime implicant is a prime implicant that is the only one covering some particular 1. That cell has to be covered by something, and only this group can do it, so every minimal expression contains this term. No choice is involved.

In the running example the prime implicants are A′B′, A′C, BC. The essentials are A′B′ and BC: cell 0 lies in no other group, and neither does cell 7. A′C is prime — it cannot grow — but not essential, since both its cells belong to other groups as well.

The procedure
  1. Find all the prime implicants: for each 1, grow the largest legal groups containing it.
  2. Mark the essential ones — look for a 1 that only one group covers. Those terms are in the answer, whatever else happens.
  3. Cover any 1s the essentials missed, using as few of the remaining prime implicants as you can.
  4. Stop. Every 1 covered, nothing redundant.

Step 2 is what most people skip, and it is the step that makes the result trustworthy. Circling greedily from the top-left corner can and does produce a term you did not need.

Usually the essentials cover everything and the answer is forced. Sometimes step 3 has a genuine choice, and then a function has more than one minimal expression — equally small, equally correct. Exercise 6.10 is a map where no prime implicant is essential and there are two different right answers, which is worth seeing once so that a disagreement with a classmate does not panic you.

6.4Don’t-cares

Some input combinations never happen. A decimal digit stored in four bits is never 1010; two sensors on the same shaft are never both active; a state encoding may leave codes unused. For those rows the output is nobody's business, and the truth table is written with an X rather than a 0 or a 1.

On the map an X is a wildcard, and the rule is the useful one:

Include an X in a group when it makes the group bigger. Ignore it otherwise.

You are choosing, per cell, whether that input produces a 0 or a 1 — and since the input never occurs, either choice is correct. You may as well choose whichever makes the circuit smaller. An X you never circle is simply being treated as a 0.

Worked example

F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(2, 3, 4, 5) + d(6, 7), where the last two rows cannot occur.

Both Xs get absorbed. Cells 2 and 3 grow upward into 6 and 7 to make a group of four, and cells 4 and 5 grow rightward into 6 and 7 to make another:

F = A′C + A′B

Treat the Xs as 0s instead and the best available is

F = A′B′C + A′BC′

— the same number of gates, but each AND gate now needs three inputs instead of two. The don't-cares were worth two gate inputs for free, and on a bigger map they routinely save whole terms.

The one thing to hold on to: those Xs are not zeros and they are not ones. Writing 0 in an X cell is a decision you were not asked to make, and it is the decision that costs you marks.

6.5Grouping the zeros

Everything so far produced a sum of products, because groups of 1s are product terms. Group the 0s instead and, with one twist, you get a minimal product of sums — the other form from Set 5, and sometimes the cheaper one.

The method: circle the 0s by exactly the same rules, read each group as a product term in the usual way, and then complement it — every literal flips and the product becomes a sum. Grouping the 0s of F is grouping the 1s of F′, so this is De Morgan applied one group at a time.

Worked example

The same running function, F = Σm(0, 1, 3, 7), now with its 0s circled:

The 0s are in cells 2, 4, 5 and 6. Two groups: cells 2 and 6 sit one above the other in the rightmost column, BC = 10, where B is 1 and C is 0 and A changes, giving BC′; cells 4 and 5 sit side by side in the A = 1 row where B is 0, giving AB′. So

F′ = BC′ + AB′

Complement each term and turn the sum into a product:

F = (B′ + C)(A′ + B)

Check it against the SOP answer from §6.2, F = A′B′ + BC, on row 5 (ABC = 101): the SOP gives 0 + 0 = 0, and the POS gives (0 + 1)(0 + 0) = 1 · 0 = 0. Agreed.

Here both forms cost two terms and four literals, so neither wins. When the map has many more 1s than 0s the POS is usually the cheaper of the two, and the count of each is visible at a glance — which is the practical reason to know this method rather than only the SOP one.

6.6Reference and pitfalls

  • Label order is Gray code, 00 01 11 10. Writing 00 01 10 11 breaks every adjacency and is the single most common way to get a K-map wrong.
  • Group sizes are powers of two, in rectangles. A group of 2 removes one variable, of 4 removes two, of 8 removes three.
  • Edges wrap, both horizontally and vertically. Corners count.
  • Diagonals are not adjacent.
  • Overlap is free; redundancy is not. Cover each 1 at least once, and drop any group whose cells are all covered by others.
  • Don't-cares join a group when they enlarge it and are ignored otherwise.
  • 1s give SOP, 0s give POS — and the 0-groups must be complemented as you read them.
  • Check your answer by evaluating one or two rows, or by expanding the minimal form back to minterms and comparing index lists.
Two traps worth naming

Reading the term backwards. A group sitting in the region where B = 0 contributes B′, not B. Check one cell of the group against the edge labels before you write the literal down.

Stopping at the first cover that works. A cover with all the 1s circled is not automatically minimal. Find the prime implicants first, then the essentials, then fill in. Greedy circling is how a three-term answer gets written for a two-term function.

6.7Exercises

Draw each map by hand before opening the solution — the drawing is half the skill, and a map with the labels in the wrong order cannot be rescued by careful grouping. Every answer here was checked mechanically, so if yours differs and you are sure of it, check the label order first.

Exercises 6.13 to 6.19 are all about don’t-cares, which are the part of this topic that is least like anything you have done before and the part the midterm leans on hardest. If you are short of time, do those.

Building and reading a map

List every cell adjacent to cell 5 of a four-variable map, and every cell adjacent to cell 0. Then explain why cells 5 and 10 are not adjacent even though they sit diagonally opposite in the middle of the map.

Two cells of a map are adjacent when their numbers differ in exactly one bit, so the neighbours of a cell are found by flipping each bit of its number in turn.

Cell 5 is 0101. Flipping each bit gives 1101, 0001, 0111, 0100 — that is 13, 1, 7 and 4.

Cell 0 is 0000, giving 1000, 0100, 0010, 0001 — 8, 4, 2 and 1. Two of those, 8 and 2, are the wrap-around neighbours: on the drawn map they sit at the opposite end of cell 0's row and column. Every cell of a four-variable map has exactly four neighbours, edge cells included — that is the sense in which the map has no edges.

Cells 5 and 10 are 0101 and 1010, which differ in all four bits. They are as far apart as two cells can be. Physical closeness on the paper is not the test; the bit patterns are. A diagonal step is always two bit flips at once, and the combining law needs exactly one.

The habit worth building: when you are unsure whether two cells may be grouped, write their numbers in binary and count the differing bits. One bit means a legal pair; anything else means no.

Map and minimise F(A, B) = Σm(0, 1, 3).

Two groups, each a pair. The top row (cells 0 and 1) has A = 0 throughout while B changes, giving A′. The right column (cells 1 and 3) has B = 1 throughout while A changes, giving B.

F = A′ + B

Cell 1 is in both groups, which is allowed and in fact unavoidable here. Overlap never costs anything: the OR gate does not care how many of its inputs are 1.

Sanity check against the definition: the only cell that is 0 is cell 2, that is A = 1, B = 0 — and A′ + B is indeed 0 exactly when A is 1 and B is 0.

Fill a map for F(A, B, C) = A′B + BC′ + AB′C directly from the expression, without building a truth table first. Then minimise it.

Each product term marks the cells where it is 1, and a term with a variable missing marks more than one cell. Take them one at a time:

  • A′B — A = 0, B = 1, C free, so cells 010 and 011: 2 and 3.
  • BC′ — B = 1, C = 0, A free, so cells 010 and 110: 2 and 6.
  • AB′C — all three fixed, so the single cell 101: 5.

Cell 2 gets marked twice, which changes nothing. The 1s are in cells 2, 3, 5 and 6, so F = Σm(2, 3, 5, 6).

Now group. Cells 2 and 3 are a pair, cells 2 and 6 are a pair, and cell 5 sits alone with no neighbour that is 1 — its neighbours are cells 1, 4 and 7, all 0. So

F = BC′ + A′B + AB′C

which is the expression you started with. It was already minimal, and the map has told you so.

That is a result worth having. Algebra can tell you an expression is smaller than another, but it can never tell you that nothing more will come off — you just run out of ideas and hope. A map settles the question: every group is as large as it can be, every 1 is covered, and there is nothing left to try.

Note also that marking cells from an expression is faster than building the truth table, and the two routes have to agree. If you are unsure of a term, expand it to minterms as in Exercise 5.5 and check the cell numbers.

Map and minimise F(A, B, C) = Σm(0, 1, 4, 5).

All four 1s form a single group: they occupy both rows, in the two columns where B = 0. A changes, C changes, B is 0 throughout.

F = B′

One literal, no gates at all beyond a single inverter. Four minterms with twelve literals between them collapsed because the group of four deletes two variables.

The columns involved are 00 and 01, which are side by side, so the block is an honest 2×2 rectangle. Had the 1s been in columns 00 and 11 the cells would not have been adjacent and nothing would have combined — another reason the Gray ordering has to be right before you start.

Map and minimise F(A, B, C) = Σm(0, 2, 4, 6). This one needs the wrap-around.

The 1s are in columns 00 and 10 — the leftmost and rightmost columns, which are drawn at opposite ends of the map and are nevertheless adjacent, since 00 and 10 differ only in B. Wrapping the map into a cylinder puts them side by side, and all four cells form one group of four.

Across it B changes and A changes; C is 0 in every cell.

F = C′

Which is obvious in hindsight: the four cells are exactly the even numbers, and an even minterm index is one whose last bit — C — is 0.

Drawn as two separate pairs the answer would have come out as A′C′ + AC′, which is correct but not minimal, and one more application of the combining law finishes it off. The map is meant to save you that second step; taking the wrap seriously is how it does.

Four variables

Map and minimise F(A, B, C) = Σm(1, 2, 5, 6). What is the largest group you can find, and why?

The largest group is a pair, and there are only two of them:

F = B′C + BC′

No group of four exists. The 1s fill two whole columns — BC = 01 and BC = 10 — and those two columns are not next to each other, since the column BC = 11 sits between them and they are not neighbours round the wrap either. Check the numbers if you doubt it: cells 1 and 2 differ in two bits, and so do 1 and 6, and 2 and 5. Nothing merges across.

The reason is visible in the expression. B′C + BC′ is the "B and C differ" function — the XOR of B and C, with A playing no part at all. Exercise 5.13 showed the same thing from the other side: a function of this kind has minterms that are pairwise two bits apart, so two-level minimisation gets no purchase on it and the canonical form is already as small as an SOP can be.

That is a real limitation rather than a failure of technique. As an SOP this needs two AND gates and an OR; with an XOR gate it is one gate. Minimal-in-two-levels and minimal-overall are different questions.

Map and minimise F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(0, 2, 8, 10).

The four 1s are in the four corners of the map, and the corners are all mutually adjacent: wrapping top to bottom and left to right brings them together into a single 2×2 block. Check the numbers — 0 and 2 differ in one bit, 0 and 8 differ in one bit, and so on round the four.

Across the group A changes and C changes; B is 0 in all four cells and so is D.

F = B′D′

This is the pattern the wrap-around rule exists for, and the one most often missed in an exam. Four corners always form a group. Two corners on the same edge always form a pair.

The check: B′D′ should be 1 exactly when B and D are both 0, which is minterms 0000, 0010, 1000 and 1010 — cells 0, 2, 8 and 10, exactly the given list.

Map and minimise F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(4, 5, 6, 7, 12, 13, 14, 15).

Eight 1s, filling the two middle rows of the map — the rows labelled AB = 01 and AB = 11. A group of eight is legal, and across it A changes, C changes and D changes, leaving only

F = B

A group of 8 in a 16-cell map removes three of the four variables, which is why the answer is a single literal and the circuit is a wire from B.

Note that those two rows are adjacent because the row labels are in Gray order: 01 and 11 differ only in A. In a map mislabelled 00, 01, 10, 11 the 1s would be in rows 2 and 4, which are not adjacent in that ordering, and this eight-cell group would be invisible. It is the clearest demonstration of why the ordering matters.

Half the table being 1 for a single-variable answer is the expected ratio: B is 1 in exactly half of all input combinations.

Map and minimise F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(0, 1, 4, 5, 10, 11, 14, 15). Describe what the function does in one sentence.

Two groups of four. The top-left block, cells 0, 1, 4 and 5, has A = 0 and C = 0 throughout, with B and D changing. The bottom-right block, cells 10, 11, 14 and 15, has A = 1 and C = 1.

F = A′C′ + AC

In words: the output is 1 exactly when A and C are equal, and B and D are irrelevant. Both have vanished from the expression, which is the map telling you that two of the four inputs do not matter.

Both blocks are ordinary 2×2 squares on the drawn map, which makes this an easy one; the thing to carry away is what a quad costs. Four cells means two variables deleted, so each term has two literals rather than four, and the pair of quads replaces eight minterms of four literals each.

The complement is worth noticing too: F is 0 exactly when A and C differ, so F′ = A′C + AC′, the same "they differ" shape as Exercise 6.5.

Prime implicants

For F(A, B, C) = Σm(0, 1, 2, 5, 6, 7), list every prime implicant, identify the essential ones, and give a minimal SOP. How many different minimal answers are there?

The prime implicants — every pair of adjacent 1s, and no group of four exists:

A′C′, A′B′, B′C, BC′, AC, AB

Six of them, each covering two cells.

The essential ones: none. Check each 1 in turn. Cell 0 is covered by A′C′ and by A′B′; cell 1 by A′B′ and B′C; cell 2 by A′C′ and BC′; and so on round the map. Every cell has two prime implicants covering it, so no group is forced.

Minimal answers. Three groups are needed — six cells, two per group — and there are two ways to choose them:

F = A′C′ + B′C + AB   or   F = A′B′ + BC′ + AC

Both use three terms and six literals. Neither is better; they are alternate minimal covers, and a map like this is called cyclic because the prime implicants chain around it in a ring with no place to start.

What to do when you meet one: pick either. If an exam answer differs from yours in a case like this, check the cost — same number of terms and literals means both are right. Only when the costs differ has someone made a mistake.

The map also shows why greedy circling is risky. Start at cell 0, take A′B′, then take BC′ for cell 2, then AC for cell 5, and cells 6 and 7 are still uncovered — you have painted yourself into a four-group answer. Cyclic maps punish improvisation.

For F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(0, 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 13), find the prime implicants, mark the essential ones, and give the minimal SOP. Show that the essentials do not cover everything.

Prime implicants: B′D′, B′C′, C′D, A′CD′, A′BD, A′BC.

Essential: B′D′ and C′D — and it is worth seeing exactly which cell forces each. The 1 in cell 10 belongs to no prime implicant except B′D′, so that term must be in the answer. The 1 in cell 13 belongs to no prime implicant except C′D. Every other 1 on this map lies in at least two prime implicants, so it forces nothing by itself — cell 0, for instance, is in both B′D′ and B′C′.

What they miss. B′D′ accounts for cells 0, 2, 8 and 10; C′D accounts for 1, 5, 9 and 13. Between them that is eight of the ten 1s, and cells 6 and 7 are still uncovered.

Those two are covered by A′BC, and adding it finishes the job:

F = B′D′ + C′D + A′BC

Three terms, and this time the answer is unique.

The exercise is the procedure in miniature. Circling the two essentials is mechanical; the only judgement needed is in the last step, and by then the problem has shrunk to two cells. That is the value of finding the essentials first — not that it avoids choices, but that it shrinks the part where choices exist.

The non-essential prime implicants are B′C′, A′CD′, A′BD, A′BC — and note that one of them, A′BC, is in the answer anyway. Non-essential does not mean useless; it means not forced. Cells 6 and 7 could equally have been covered by A′CD′ together with A′BD, which is correct but costs two terms instead of one. B′C′ is the only prime implicant here that earns nothing at all: everything it covers is already covered by the two essentials.

A classmate offers this cover for F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 11, 14, 15): every group is a legal prime implicant and every 1 is covered. Is it minimal? If not, fix it.

The proposed answer, drawn out:

F = A′C′ + A′B′ + B′C + AC

All four groups are genuine prime implicants and every 1 does lie in at least one of them, so the expression is correct. It is not minimal.

Work the procedure. The prime implicants are A′C′, A′B′, B′C, AC. Now look for cells that only one of them covers:

  • Cell 4 lies only in A′C′, so A′C′ is essential.
  • Cells 14 and 15 lie only in AC, so AC is essential.
  • Every other 1 lies in two prime implicants, so nothing else is forced.

Those two essentials cover 0, 1, 4, 5, 10, 11, 14 and 15 — everything except cells 2 and 3. One more group finishes the job, and there are two candidates, A′B′ and B′C, either of which covers both:

F = A′C′ + A′B′ + AC   or   F = A′C′ + B′C + AC

Three terms, not four. The classmate's cover used both of the optional groups when either alone would do.

This is the characteristic failure of circling greedily: each group taken was reasonable on its own, and no rule was broken, and the result is still one gate too big. The check that catches it costs ten seconds — take each group in turn and ask whether every one of its cells is covered by some other group. If the answer is yes, delete it.

Don’t-cares

Minimise F(A, B, C) = Σm(1, 3) + d(5, 7), where the inputs marked X never occur. What would the answer be if you treated the Xs as 0s?

The two 1s are cells 1 and 3, and the two Xs are directly below them at cells 5 and 7. Taking both Xs into the group makes one block of four covering the whole C = 1 column pair:

F = C

A single wire from C.

Treating the Xs as 0s leaves cells 1 and 3 as a pair on their own, covering the A = 0 row where C = 1, so F = A′C — an AND gate and an inverter instead of nothing at all.

Both circuits are correct, because they differ only on inputs that never arrive. The second one just pays for a distinction nobody asked for. That is the whole argument for using don't-cares, and it is why an X left uncircled should be a deliberate decision rather than an oversight.

F(A, B, C) = Σm(1, 5) + d(0, 4, 7). Which of the don’t-cares end up inside a group and which does not? Give the minimal SOP, and the answer you would get by treating every X as 0.

The two 1s are cells 1 and 5. Look at what each X could do for them:

  • The X in cell 0 sits beside cell 1, and the X in cell 4 sits beside cell 5. Taking both turns the pair {1, 5} into the block {0, 1, 4, 5} — four cells, spanning both rows and the two columns where B = 0.
  • The X in cell 7 is adjacent to cell 5, and the pair {5, 7} gives AC. But cell 5 is already inside the block above, and cell 7 is an X that nobody needs covered. Circling it would add a gate to cover nothing.

F = B′

So two of the three don't-cares are used and one is not — and the one left out is simply being treated as a 0, which is a legitimate choice because that input never arrives.

Treating every X as 0 leaves cells 1 and 5 as a pair on their own, in the column where B = 0 and C = 1:

F = B′C

An AND gate and two inverters, where the don't-cares bought you a single inverter.

The rule from §6.4 in action: include an X when it makes a group bigger, ignore it otherwise. There is no penalty for ignoring one, and no prize for circling them all. A student who dutifully covers every X on the map will often produce a larger circuit than one who covers none.

Three position sensors A, B and C are mounted on a rotating shaft, and the mechanism guarantees that at most one of them is ever active at a time. Design a circuit that outputs 1 when sensor A or sensor B is active. Give the Σ and d lists and the minimal SOP.

Setting up the lists. An input with two or more sensors active cannot occur, so those rows are don't-cares: 011, 101, 110 and 111, that is cells 3, 5, 6 and 7.

Of the rows that can occur, the output is 1 when A is active (cell 4, that is 100) or B is active (cell 2, that is 010). The row 000 — no sensor active at all — is a perfectly legal input, and the output there should be 0, not a don't-care. So does cell 1, where only C is active.

F(A, B, C) = Σm(2, 4) + d(3, 5, 6, 7)

Both groups grow to four cells by absorbing don't-cares. The row A = 1 gives A, and the two columns where B = 1 give B:

F = B + A

A single OR gate, which in hindsight is exactly what the specification says: output 1 if A or B is on. The don't-cares let the map discover the obvious answer instead of an elaborate one.

Without them — that is, if the illegal combinations had to output 0 — the two 1s are isolated and neither pairs with anything:

F = A′BC′ + AB′C′

Two three-input AND gates, four inverters and an OR, to compute what one OR gate computes. That circuit spends all its effort on inputs that never happen.

The judgement call is whether to trust the guarantee, and it is the same question as in Exercise 6.16. Here the guarantee is mechanical — one shaft, one lobe, sensors that physically cannot overlap — which is about as trustworthy as a guarantee gets. A guarantee that rests on another engineer remembering something is worth less.

A four-bit input A B C D holds a single decimal digit in binary-coded decimal — the digit 0 to 9 written in binary, four bits to a digit, so that a multi-digit number keeps one four-bit group per digit. Only 0000 to 1001 are used, and the codes 1010 to 1111 never occur. Design a circuit that outputs 1 when the digit is prime. Give the Σ and d lists, and the minimal SOP.

The one-digit primes are 2, 3, 5 and 7, so

F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(2, 3, 5, 7) + d(10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15)

(1 is not prime, and 9 is 3×3.)

Two groups, both reaching into the don't-care region:

  • Cells 2 and 3 extend down into the Xs at 10 and 11, giving a block of four in which B = 0 and C = 1 — the term B′C.
  • Cells 5 and 7 extend down into the Xs at 13 and 15, giving a block in which B = 1 and D = 1 — the term BD.

F = B′C + BD

Two AND gates and an OR, with no inverter needed except on B.

Worth checking that this does the right thing on the legal inputs only. It claims F = 1 for 1010 and 1011 — decimal 10 and 11 — which as it happens is right for 11 and wrong for 10, and neither matters, because a binary-coded decimal digit is never those values. Outside its specification the circuit may do anything at all, and exploiting that freedom is exactly what made it small.

If the input were not guaranteed to be a single decimal digit, this would be a bug, and a serious one. Don't-cares are a contract: they are only safe as long as the guarantee that produced them holds. When a circuit like this is fed from somewhere new, the assumption has to be rechecked.

Redo Exercise 6.16 with no don't-cares — that is, the six illegal codes must produce 0. Compare the two circuits.

With the bottom half of the map forced to 0, neither group can grow downward. Cells 2 and 3 pair off, and cells 5 and 7 pair off, but each pair now needs three literals to pin it down:

F = A′B′C + A′BD

The comparison. Both answers have two product terms and therefore the same gate count — two ANDs and an OR. But the don't-care version uses two-input ANDs and one inverter, while this one needs three-input ANDs and two inverters: 6 literals against 4.

So the saving here is modest, and it is worth being honest that it usually is on a map this small. The reason to reach for don't-cares is not the typical case but the occasional one where six wildcards let a whole term disappear — and the fact that the saving is free either way.

There is also a design question hiding in the comparison. This second circuit is safer: fed an illegal code it outputs 0 rather than something arbitrary. If the guarantee about the input being a decimal digit is one you actually trust, take the smaller circuit; if it comes from another team's hardware, the extra inverter may be cheap insurance.

F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(0, 4, 12, 14) + d(2, 6, 8, 10). Minimise it, then minimise it again with the don’t-cares forced to 0, and compare.

The four 1s and the four Xs together fill every cell where D = 0 — cells 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 14, which are exactly the even-numbered cells. That is a group of eight, and a group of eight in a sixteen-cell map deletes three variables:

F = D′

One literal. The circuit is an inverter on D, and A, B and C are not connected to anything.

With the Xs forced to 0, the four 1s cannot merge into anything that large. Cells 0 and 4 pair, cells 12 and 14 pair, and the best available is

F = A′C′D′ + ABD′

Two terms, six literals: two three-input ANDs, an OR and four inverters, against one inverter.

This is the case Exercise 6.17 said was worth waiting for. There, four don't-cares saved two gate inputs; here, four of them delete an entire product term and three of the four inputs. The difference is where the Xs happen to fall — scattered among the 1s so that they bridge groups that could not otherwise reach each other.

Which is the practical lesson about don't-cares. You cannot tell in advance whether they will save you a little or a lot, and the cost of finding out is zero: put the Xs on the map and let the grouping tell you.

F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(2, 3, 6, 7, 10, 11) + d(0, 1, 4, 5). Find the minimal POS by grouping the 0s — an X may join a 0-group just as readily as a 1-group. Then find the minimal SOP and compare the two answers on cell 0.

Grouping the 0s. The 0s are in cells 8, 9, 12, 13, 14 and 15. Treat the four Xs as 0s wherever that enlarges a group:

Cells 12, 13, 14, 15 are the row where A = 1 and B = 1, giving AB. And cells 8, 9, 12, 13 extend leftward into the Xs at 0, 1, 4 and 5 to make a group of eight — every cell where C = 0 — giving C′. So

F′ = C′ + AB

Complement each term and turn the sum into a product:

F = (C)(A′ + B′)

Grouping the 1s on the same map, with the Xs now recruited into 1-groups instead:

F = A′ + B′C

Two terms and three literals either way, so on cost there is nothing to choose between them.

But look at cell 0, which is a don't-care. The SOP answer A′ + B′C gives A′ = 1 there, so it outputs 1. The POS answer C(A′ + B′) has C = 0 there, so it outputs 0.

The two circuits compute different functions — and both are correct answers to the question. That is what a don't-care means: on those rows the specification does not say, and any two circuits that agree everywhere it does say are equally valid solutions.

The practical consequence is one to be careful about. Each X is resolved to a 0 or a 1 by whichever groups you drew, and the choice is made independently for each form. So you cannot check an SOP answer against a POS answer row by row and expect agreement — they will differ on don't-care rows. Compare them only on the rows the specification actually pins down, or compare each separately against the original Σ and d lists.

Zeros, and beyond the map

For F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15), find the minimal SOP by grouping the 1s and the minimal POS by grouping the 0s. Which circuit is cheaper?

Grouping the 1s.

The whole A = 1 half of the map is 1, which is a group of eight giving the single literal A. That leaves cells 4 and 6, which pair with each other — both have A = 0, B = 1, D = 0 — but they also pair into the covered half: cells 4 and 12 have B = 1, C = 0, D = 0, and 6 and 14 likewise. Taking the largest group containing them gives the four cells 4, 6, 12 and 14, where B = 1 and D = 0.

F = BD′ + A

Two terms, three literals. Note that BD′ overlaps the A group in cells 12 and 14, which is free and is what let the group grow from a pair to a quad.

Grouping the 0s.

The 0s are in cells 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7. Two groups of four: cells 0, 1, 2 and 3 are the top row, where A = 0 and B = 0; cells 1, 3, 5 and 7 form the block where A = 0 and D = 1. So

F′ = A′B′ + A′D

Complementing each term and turning the sum into a product:

F = (A + B)(A + D′)

Which is cheaper. The SOP is 3 literals in 2 terms; the POS is 4 literals in 2 terms. The SOP wins, by one gate input.

It is worth doing both and then choosing, because there is no way to tell in advance which will come out ahead — counting 1s against 0s is a useful hint and not a rule. Here the counts are 10 against 6, which would have suggested the POS, and the suggestion is wrong.

Cross-check the two forms on cell 4 (ABCD = 0100), which should be 1: the SOP gives 0 + 1 · 1 = 1, and the POS gives (0 + 1)(0 + 1) = 1. Agreed.

Challenge. Explain why a group on a K-map must contain a power-of-two number of cells. Then explain why the method is not used beyond five or six variables, and say what replaces it.

Why powers of two. Start from the combining law: two terms merge into one only when they agree in every variable but one, and the result is the shared part with that variable deleted. So merging is always a doubling — a term covering k cells becomes a term covering 2k.

Build a group by repeated merging, starting from a single cell. After one merge it covers 2 cells, after two merges 4, after three 8. A term with n − j literals in an n-variable function is 1 on exactly 2j input combinations, because the j missing variables are each free to take either value. There is no product term that is 1 on exactly six inputs, and so no legal group of six cells — not because the rule forbids it, but because no expression could be read off it.

The same argument says why the group must be a rectangle in the map's coordinates: the set of cells where a product term is 1 is exactly the set obtained by letting the absent variables range freely, which is a subcube, which the Gray ordering draws as a rectangle.

Why the method runs out. Two reasons, and the first is the one you feel immediately. A map for n variables has 2n cells — 16 for four variables, 32 for five, 64 for six — and each cell has n neighbours. A five-variable map is drawn as two four-variable maps with cells adjacent between the sheets; a six-variable map needs four. Adjacency stops being something you can see, which is the whole reason the map existed.

The second reason is deeper. Finding a minimum cover is not just visually hard, it is computationally hard — it is the set-covering problem, which is NP-complete, so no method is going to stay easy as the number of variables grows. The map is a way of solving small instances by eye, not a way of making the problem small.

What replaces it. The Quine–McCluskey algorithm does exactly what you have been doing by hand — find all prime implicants by repeated merging, then choose a minimum cover — but as a tabular procedure a computer can follow, with no adjacency to see. It is exact and it is exponential, so it in turn gives way to heuristics such as espresso, which produce very good but not provably minimal covers on functions with hundreds of inputs. Every logic synthesis tool in use today runs some descendant of espresso, and the K-map is what it is doing, scaled up past the point where eyes work.

Worth knowing

The prime implicants, the essential ones and the minimal covers printed in the solutions on this page were generated by a Quine–McCluskey implementation and checked against the truth table row by row. That is a reasonable thing to do for your own homework too — not to find the answer, but to confirm one you have already found by hand.
